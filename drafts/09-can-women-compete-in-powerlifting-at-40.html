Can Women Compete in Powerlifting at 40?

Yes, and the masters divisions are built for exactly that. Competition records, training expectations, and the reality of stepping on the platform for the first time at 40.

Center Mass Strength

Every major powerlifting federation has masters divisions for women, and most of them start at age 40. Women's masters powerlifting is one of the fastest-growing parts of the sport, with records in every age bracket through the 60s and beyond. The question isn't really whether women can compete at 40. It's what competing actually looks like, and whether it's for you.

The data point that surprises most people: in the sport's longitudinal records, women tend to show faster early strength gains than men, largely because female beginners start from lower baselines and the nervous system responds quickly to structured training (Latella et al., 2024). A 40-year-old woman starting powerlifting is not behind; she is at the beginning of the same curve everyone else starts on, and the early returns are often dramatic.

What this guide covers: the masters division structure, how strong you actually need to be, what training looks like for women over 40, the health reasons that make lifting a strong choice, and how to enter your first meet.

Masters Divisions: Built for the Over-40 Lifter

The structure is straightforward. Federations differ on exact labels, but the common pattern is five-year age bands:

DivisionAge
Masters 140-44
Masters 245-49
Masters 350-54
Masters 455-59
Masters 5 and up60 and beyond

You always also compete within your weight class, so you're matched with lifters of similar bodyweight and age. No, you don't get to compete only against other beginners, but you're never in the same bracket as a 25-year-old heavyweight either. Masters divisions exist precisely so the over-40 lifter competes against peers.

How Strong Do You Need to Be?

You don't need a qualifying total. Local meets are open to first-timers, and the only real requirement is that you can perform the three lifts to competition standards: depth on the squat, pause on the bench, and lockout on the deadlift. The records are held by strong lifters; the platform is open to everyone who trains.

What matters more than the number is consistency. Powerlifting rewards lifters who show up for years, and the women who succeed in the masters divisions are usually the ones who built a sustainable training habit in their 40s and never stopped. The full case for what consistent training produces over years is in Strength Training Expectations After 40.

What Training Looks Like for Women Over 40

The training is the same sport everyone else is training for, with the same honest foundations that apply to every masters lifter:

  • A submaximal base. Most volume below failure, heavy work on fewer intentional days. The reasoning is in Submaximal Training.
  • Consistent practice of the three lifts. Technique is the skill that compounds; training around the lifts, not away from them.
  • Joint-aware load management. Muscle adapts faster than connective tissue at every age. The framework is in Your Joints Are the Real Limit.
  • Recovery as a training input. Sleep, stress, and nutrition decide whether the training pays off. This matters more with every passing year, and the readiness-based approach is explained in Powerlifting Over 50: Why Readiness Changes Everything.

For women specifically, there is a second reason to lift that has nothing to do with the platform: resistance training is one of the most effective ways to maintain muscle mass and bone density through the menopausal transition. The research on resistance training in postmenopausal women shows meaningful improvements in strength and function, with the caveat that the evidence base still needs higher-quality trials (Sá et al., 2023). The menopause-specific training conversation joins this cluster as its own guide.

Entering Your First Meet

The path is more approachable than the Instagram clips suggest. Find a local meet through a federation's event calendar, register for the masters division, and treat the first one as a learning experience rather than a judgment. Most federations publish rules for the three lifts, and a coached prep block before the meet removes most of the uncertainty.

The meet-prep process, from picking a date to peaking and tapering, is covered end to end in the Programming & Periodization silo. Start with The Complete Guide to Powerlifting Periodization to understand how the training around a competition is structured.

Can Women Compete in Powerlifting at 40? Common Questions

Can women compete in powerlifting at 40?

Yes. Every major federation has masters divisions for women, and most start at age 40. Women's masters powerlifting is one of the fastest-growing parts of the sport, with competition records in every age bracket through the 60s and beyond. Female powerlifters also tend to show faster early strength gains than men in the sport's data.

What are the masters divisions in powerlifting?

Federations differ in the details, but the common structure is five-year age bands: masters 1 (40-44), masters 2 (45-49), masters 3 (50-54), masters 4 (55-59), and masters 5 (60-64), with some federations continuing through 70 and older. You always also compete within your weight class, so you're matched with lifters of similar bodyweight and age.

How strong do I need to be to compete at 40?

You don't need a qualifying total. Local meets are open to first-timers, and the only real requirement is that you can perform the three lifts to competition standards: depth on the squat, pause on the bench, and lockout on the deadlift. The records are held by strong lifters, but the platform is open to everyone who trains.

What should women over 40 focus on in powerlifting training?

The same foundations that serve every masters lifter, with the addition of targeted strength work: a submaximal base, consistent squat/bench/deadlift practice, joint-aware load management, and recovery treated as a training input. For women in their 40s, maintaining muscle and bone density through resistance training is also a meaningful health reason to lift.
