← The Strength Blog 5/3/1 POWERLIFTING PROGRAM · THE COMPLETE GUIDE 5/3/1 Is Simple. That Is the Whole Point.

One training max, four lifts, four-week cycles. The 5/3/1 program has outlasted every trend in powerlifting because it asks for less than you think and delivers it for longer than you expect. Here is the whole system — the cycle, the templates, and how to run it for years.

Center Mass Strength

The 5/3/1 powerlifting program is the most run program in the world, and the reason is not that it is the most effective. It is that it is the most followable. Jim Wendler published it in 2009, and the structure has barely changed since: four lifts, one training max, and a four-week wave that repeats, month after month, for as long as you run it. There is no spreadsheet to maintain, no daily decision to make, and no week where you have to guess what the weight should be. You walk in, you look at the number, you lift it. That simplicity is not a limitation of the program. It is the program. The lifters who get the most out of 5/3/1 are not the ones who understand it best. They are the ones who run it longest without changing it.

This guide covers the whole system: the training max and why it is set below your real max, the four-week cycle with the exact percentages, the two templates that matter most — Boring But Big and First Set Last — the assistance work that supports them, the beginner version, and the mistakes that turn a good program into a stalled one. If you are still choosing between the famous programs, the honest comparison of the best powerlifting programs and how to pick the one that fits you puts 5/3/1 next to Sheiko, Candito, GZCL, the Texas Method, and Juggernaut. This post is the deep dive on one of them: what 5/3/1 is, how it works, and how to run it properly.

The short version

5/3/1 is four lifts, one training max, and a four-week wave. You train the squat, bench press, deadlift, and overhead press, one per day, four days a week. Every percentage comes from a training max set at 90 percent of your true one-rep max — not from the max itself. Week one is sets of 5, week two is sets of 3, week three is the 5/3/1 week with a heavy single, and week four is a deload. After each cycle you add five pounds to the upper-body training maxes and ten pounds to the lower-body ones, and you run it again. The weights are light on purpose. The week-three top set is 95 percent of the training max, which is about 85 percent of your true max — a weight you can move with speed and good form, week after week, for years. The progression is monthly, not weekly, and that is the feature. Five pounds a month on the bench and ten on the squat does not sound like much, and that is the point: it is progress you can actually sustain, and a year of it is sixty pounds on the press and a hundred and twenty on the squat and deadlift, if the cycles hold.

What 5/3/1 actually is

Jim Wendler was an elite powerlifter who got tired of programs that asked for everything every week. He built 5/3/1 around a simple observation: most lifters do not fail because the program is too easy. They fail because the program is too hard to sustain, or too complicated to follow when life gets in the way, or too aggressive about the weights for a lifter who has a job, a family, and joints that remember every bad decision. So he built a program that is deliberately conservative about the weight, deliberately simple about the structure, and deliberately patient about the progression. The original book came out in 2009. Beyond 5/3/1 followed in 2013, and 5/3/1 Forever in 2017 added the leader and anchor cycles that organize the templates into longer blocks. The core has not changed in any of them, because the core is the point: a training max below your real max, a four-week wave, and a small, regular increase.

The program trains four lifts. Squat, bench press, deadlift, and overhead press — the three competition lifts plus the press, which Wendler kept because a lifter who can press overhead is a lifter whose shoulders and upper back are doing real work. Each lift gets one day a week in the standard four-day split. That is the whole schedule, and it is the first thing lifters get wrong about 5/3/1: they look at four days, one lift each, and think it is not enough. It is enough, because the supplemental and assistance work fills the rest of the session, and because a program you can run for a year beats a program you can run for six weeks. The complete guide to powerlifting periodization and how training blocks fit together shows the structure underneath: build volume, push intensity, peak, test, repeat — and 5/3/1 is that shape drawn in the simplest possible way, one four-week wave at a time.

The training max: the number everything hangs on

The training max is the most important idea in 5/3/1, and the one most lifters get wrong first. Your training max is not your one-rep max. It is a planning number set at 90 percent of your true max — Wendler's later guidance for beginners uses 85 percent, and the rule in every version is the same: err on the side of too light. Every percentage in the program is calculated from the training max, not from your real max. So a lifter with a 400-pound squat sets a training max of 360, and the week-three top set at 95 percent of the training max is 342 pounds — about 85 percent of the true max. That is a weight you can move with speed, with good form, for more than the prescribed rep. And that is the point.

The reason is not caution. It is arithmetic. A program that asks you to train at 95 percent of your true max every third week will stall or break you within a few cycles, because there is no room for a bad day, a bad night of sleep, or a week where work ran long. A program that asks you to train at 85 percent of your true max has room for all of those things, and it still builds strength, because strength is built by accumulating good reps at weights you can move well — not by grinding reps at weights you can barely move. The guide to submaximal training and why you should not max out all the time explains the principle in full, and 5/3/1 is the program that principle built. The weights feel light in week one. They are supposed to. The test is not whether the first cycle feels hard. The test is whether the twelfth cycle is still moving.

Setting the training max is simple. Take your best recent max — a tested single, or an estimated max from a heavy set of three to five reps — and take 90 percent of it. If you have not tested a max recently, use a rep-max estimate and be conservative: a training max you can hit for five clean reps is a training max you can build on, and a training max you can barely hit for one rep is a training max that will stall you. If you are new to the program, or coming back from time off, use 85 percent. The cost of starting too light is a few weeks of easy training. The cost of starting too heavy is a stalled program and a reset. Start light. The program will catch up with you, and it will do it faster than you think.

The four-week cycle, with the exact percentages

Every cycle is four weeks. Three working weeks, then a deload. The percentages are of the training max, and the plus sign on the last set means as many good reps as you have got — the AMRAP set, which is where the program measures progress.

WeekSet 1Set 2Set 3 (top set)Focus
Week 1 (5s)65% × 575% × 585% × 5+Volume base, movement rhythm
Week 2 (3s)70% × 380% × 390% × 3+Moderate intensity, moderate volume
Week 3 (5/3/1)75% × 585% × 395% × 1+Heavy technical peak
Week 4 (deload)40% × 550% × 560% × 5Fatigue dissipation, no AMRAP

Read the table by the top set, because the top set is the set that matters. In week one the top set is 85 percent of the training max for five or more reps. In week two it is 90 percent for three or more. In week three it is 95 percent for one or more. The AMRAP reps are the program's feedback loop: if you get eight reps at 85 percent in week one, the training max is right, or maybe a little light. If you get five, it is right. If you get three, it is too heavy, and the cycle will tell you that again in week three, louder. The AMRAP is not a max test. It is a measurement, and the measurement only works if the reps are good ones — bar speed maintained, form held, no grinding rep that takes ten seconds and costs you the next session.

The deload week is not optional, and it is not a wasted week. It is the week that lets the previous three weeks turn into strength instead of fatigue. Three sets of five at 40, 50, and 60 percent, no AMRAP, and then the next cycle starts from a stronger place. Lifters skip the deload because it feels too easy, and then they wonder why the next cycle stalls. The deload is the program working as designed. The guide to deloads vs one-day adjustments and when to back off covers how to tell a planned deload from a bad day, and the short version is this: a bad day is one session, a deload is a week, and you should be doing the second one on schedule whether you feel like you need it or not.

Warm up before the working sets. A standard 5/3/1 warm-up is 40 percent for five, 50 percent for five, and 60 percent for three, all of the training max, then the first working set. The warm-up is not training. It is preparation, and the lifter who skips it to save ten minutes is the lifter who pulls something in week two and loses three weeks. Take the ten minutes.

Progression: five and ten pounds, every cycle

After each four-week cycle, you add weight to the training max: five pounds on the upper-body lifts (bench press and overhead press) and ten pounds on the lower-body lifts (squat and deadlift). Then you run the next cycle from the new training max. That is the entire progression rule, and it is the reason 5/3/1 works for years. Five pounds a month is sixty pounds a year on the bench and press. Ten pounds a month is a hundred and twenty a year on the squat and deadlift. No lifter adds a hundred and twenty pounds to their squat in a year after their beginner phase, of course — the training max will outrun the true max eventually, and that is when the reset rule applies. But the arithmetic shows why the program is patient: the increases are small enough to sustain, and they compound.

The reset rule is simple, and it is the rule that keeps the program honest. If you fail to hit the prescribed minimum reps on the week-three top set — if you cannot get the single at 95 percent — reduce your training max by 10 percent and rebuild. Wendler's rule is to roll back three to four months of progression, which is roughly what a 10 percent cut does after a few cycles of five- and ten-pound jumps. The reset is not a failure. It is the program working. A training max that has crept too close to your true max stops being a training max and starts being a max, and a program built on a max is a program that stalls. Reset, rebuild, and the numbers start moving again. Lifters who refuse to reset are lifters who stall for six months and then blame the program. The program told them what to do. They did not do it.

There is also a variant worth knowing: 5's PRO. In the original program, the top set is an AMRAP — as many reps as possible. In 5's PRO, you do exactly five clean reps on all three sets, no AMRAP, and the progression comes from the training max increases instead of the rep records. Wendler introduced it for lifters who were beating themselves up on the AMRAP sets, or running 5/3/1 alongside a sport, or in a leader cycle where the volume is already high. If the AMRAP sets are the part of the program you love, keep them. If they are the part that leaves you wrecked for the next session, 5's PRO is the honest alternative. The program does not care which one you pick. It cares that you pick one and run it for a full cycle before judging it.

The templates: Boring But Big and First Set Last

The main 5/3/1 sets are only the first part of the session. After them comes the supplemental work, and the supplemental template is what decides whether the program builds size, builds skill, or just maintains strength. Two templates matter most.

Boring But Big (BBB) is the most popular 5/3/1 template, and the simplest: after the main work, do five sets of ten reps of the same lift at 50 to 60 percent of your training max. Same lift, lighter weight, a lot of reps. The BBB sets build muscle, work capacity, and technical consistency — fifty reps of practice after the heavy work, at a weight you can move well. Start at 50 percent, or lower if you are new to the volume: Wendler tells lifters new to BBB to start around 30 percent, especially for squats and deadlifts, because the soreness is real. A common ramp is 50 percent in cycle one, 55 to 60 percent in cycle two, and 60 percent in cycle three. Starting too heavy on the 5×10 sets is the fastest way to miss reps, and missed reps on supplemental work are a sign the template is too heavy, not that you are too weak. Fifty percent feels light on set one. By set four and five of deadlifts, it does not.

First Set Last (FSL) is the other main template: after the main work, go back to the weight of the first working set and do more sets with it — typically five sets of five, at 65 percent in week one, 70 percent in week two, and 75 percent in week three. FSL builds volume without introducing a new load, and it is the default recommendation for most lifters, because it is the template that interferes least with recovery. It is also the template Wendler pairs with leader cycles, where the main work is 5's PRO and the volume comes from the supplemental sets instead of the AMRAP. If BBB is the template for building size, FSL is the template for building the lifts themselves — more practice at a moderate weight, with the bar speed to match.

There are other templates — Second Set Last, Joker sets (heavy singles after the top set, on days you feel strong), the Building the Monolith challenge — but BBB and FSL are the two that cover almost every lifter. Pick BBB if you want size and work capacity and can recover from the volume. Pick FSL if you want practice and sustainable progress and would rather keep the recovery cost low. Run the template for at least two full cycles before switching. A template is not a workout. It is a block, and a block needs time to show what it does.

In 5/3/1 Forever, Wendler organizes the templates into leader and anchor cycles. A leader cycle runs for two or three cycles with higher-volume supplemental work (BBB or FSL with 5's PRO, no AMRAP) to build the base. An anchor cycle runs for one cycle with lower volume and the AMRAP top sets back in, to test what the leader built. The leader builds, the anchor tests, and then the next leader starts from a stronger place. You do not need to run leader and anchor cycles to run 5/3/1 — the original program is just the four-week wave, repeated — but if you have run the basic program for a year and want more structure, leader and anchor is the structure Wendler built for you. It is the difference between running cycles and running a plan.

A sample week: the four-day split

Here is what a week looks like on the standard four-day split, with BBB as the supplemental template. One main lift per day, the supplemental work after it, and assistance work to finish the session.

DayMain lift (5/3/1)SupplementalAssistance
MondayOverhead pressPress 5×10Chins, rows, core
TuesdayDeadliftDeadlift 5×10Single-leg work, back extensions, core
ThursdayBench pressBench 5×10Rows, dumbbell press, triceps
FridaySquatSquat 5×10Single-leg work, hamstrings, core

Two notes on the split. First, the order is Wendler's default — press, deadlift, bench, squat — and the reason the deadlift and squat are separated by two days is recovery: the two heaviest lifts should not sit next to each other. If your schedule forces a different order, keep the squat and deadlift separated and the rest will sort itself out. Second, the assistance work is not the program. Wendler's own BBB examples pair upper-body days with lat work and lower-body days with ab work, and his rule is that assistance should not be trained with the same intensity as the main lifts. Assistance builds the muscle that supports the main lifts. It is not a second workout, and a lifter who turns it into one is a lifter who will not recover for the next main session. Fifty to a hundred total reps of assistance per session, at a weight you control, is the range. More is not better. More is just more.

Conditioning fits around the program, not inside it. Wendler's rule is simple: lift four days, condition on two or three of the other days, and keep the conditioning easy enough that it does not cost you the next lifting session. Walking, sled work, easy bike rides — the kind of conditioning that builds work capacity without adding joint stress. A lifter who runs hard intervals the day before a heavy squat session is not conditioning. They are sabotaging the squat, and the log will show it. For lifters over 40, this matters more, not less: recovery is the variable the program has to be solved with, and conditioning that costs recovery is conditioning that costs strength.

5/3/1 for beginners

There is a beginner version, and it is worth knowing about, because it is the right answer for a lifter who is past linear progression but not ready for the full four-day split. Wendler's 5/3/1 for Beginners uses three full-body days with two main lifts each — squat and bench on day one, deadlift and press on day two, bench and squat on day three — with the main work at 5's PRO (five clean reps, no AMRAP) and FSL 5×5 as the supplemental work. The three-day version trains each lift more often, which is what a beginner needs: practice, not variety. The training max is set even more conservatively, at 85 percent, because a beginner's max is a moving target and the program should not chase it.

The beginner version is a bridge, not a destination. A true beginner — under a year of consistent training — should run linear progression first, adding weight every session, because that is the fastest progress a beginner will ever make. The complete guide to a powerlifting program for beginners covers that phase, and the handoff from linear progression to 5/3/1 is the moment a lifter stops being a beginner. The beginner version of 5/3/1 is the handoff: same lifts, more frequency, a conservative training max, and a structure that teaches the lifter to follow a program instead of chasing a number every session. Run it for three to six months, then move to the four-day split when the weights and the schedule both say you are ready.

Who 5/3/1 is for — and who it is not for

5/3/1 is for the intermediate lifter who wants simple, sustainable progress and is willing to be patient about it. One to three years of training, a real base of strength, a schedule that allows four days a week, and a temperament that can handle a program that feels too easy in week one. It is for the lifter who has stalled on linear progression and needs a program that moves the weight up monthly instead of every session. It is for the lifter who has been hurt by programs that asked for too much, too fast, and wants a program that asks for less and delivers it for longer.

It is also, honestly, one of the best programs for lifters over 40. The reasons are the same reasons it works for anyone, but they matter more with age: the weights are submaximal, so the joints take less pounding; the progression is monthly, so there is no weekly pressure to add weight on a week when sleep or stress says otherwise; and there is a built-in deload every fourth week, which is the week that turns training into strength instead of fatigue. A 45-year-old lifter with a job, a family, and a back that remembers every bad decision needs a program that respects recovery as a variable, not as an excuse. 5/3/1 does, by design. The complete guide to an intermediate powerlifting program and the 4-day template covers the handoff from beginner to intermediate in detail, and 5/3/1 is one of the best programs to hand off to — the structure is simple enough to follow on a tired morning, and the progression is slow enough to sustain for years.

5/3/1 is not for the lifter with a meet in eight weeks. The monthly wave is too patient for a peak, and a lifter who needs a test date should run a program with a peak built in — Candito, with its week-six test, or a meet-prep cycle. It is not for the lifter who wants fast progress, or the lifter who gets bored easily, because the program is deliberately boring: the same four lifts, the same four weeks, the same small increase, month after month. And it is not for the advanced lifter whose total has outgrown the template, because at that level the program has to fit the lifter, not the other way around. The honest test is the same as for any program: run it for two or three cycles. Did the numbers go up? Then it works, whatever is inside it. The complete guide to RPE for powerlifting and rating your sets honestly is the tool for that test — a set that felt like an 8 when it was a 10 is a set that will cost you next week, and the log is what tells you whether the program is working or whether you are lying to it.

How to run it for a year

Picking the program is the easy part. Running it for a year is the work, and the rules for running 5/3/1 for a year are the same as the rules for running any program for a year, with one addition: respect the training max.

First, run it as written for at least two full cycles before changing anything. The first cycle is orientation: you are learning the structure, the weights are estimates, and the numbers are noisy. The second cycle is the test: the weights are calibrated, the structure is familiar, and the results are real. A lifter who changes a template after one cycle has not tested it. They have tried it on, and a program is not a shirt.

Second, do not add volume to a program that is working. The most common way lifters break 5/3/1 is by adding sets to the BBB work, adding a fifth day, or adding accessories, because the program feels too easy in week two. It feels easy because it is supposed to: the hard weeks are at the end of the cycle, and the easy weeks are what make the hard weeks possible. Adding volume to a program that is working is like adding weight to a bar that is already moving — it feels productive and it ruins the plan. If the program is working, leave it alone. If it is not working after two full cycles, change one thing, not five, and give it another cycle before judging.

Third, take the deload, and reset the training max when the program tells you to. The deload is not a wasted week, and the reset is not a failure. Both are the program working as designed, and the lifters who skip them are the lifters who stall for six months and then blame the program. The program told them what to do. They did not do it.

Fourth, track everything. The weight, the reps on the AMRAP set, and how the set actually felt. The AMRAP reps are the data, and the data decides whether the training max is right, whether the template is working, and whether the next cycle should go up by five and ten or stay where it is. The program is a plan. The log is the evidence. The lifter who has both is the lifter whose total goes up. And if you want the program to adjust to the log instead of to a fixed spreadsheet — the training max set from your actual numbers, the volume adjusted to how you actually recover — that is what adaptive programming does, and it is the same principles as 5/3/1, with the adjustments made for you instead of by you. The test is the same either way. Run it for two or three cycles. Did the numbers go up?

The mistakes that turn 5/3/1 bad

Starting the training max too high. The most common mistake, and the one that causes most of the others. A training max set at your true max is not a training max. It is a max, and a program built on a max stalls within two cycles. Set it at 90 percent. If you are new, or coming back, set it at 85 percent. The cost of starting too light is a few weeks of easy training. The cost of starting too heavy is a stalled program.

Grinding the AMRAP sets. The plus set is as many good reps as you have got, and the word that matters is good. A rep that takes ten seconds, with form breaking down, is not a good rep. It is a rep that costs you the next session, and the session after that. Leave one or two reps in the tank on the AMRAP sets, most of the time, and save the true all-out effort for the test at the end of a longer block. The AMRAP is a measurement, not a max test, and a measurement taken with bad form is a bad measurement.

Skipping the deload. Every lifter skips it at least once. The deload is not a wasted week. It is the week that lets the previous three weeks turn into strength instead of fatigue, and skipping it is the fastest way to turn a working program into a stalled one.

Adding volume to a working program. The BBB sets are already a lot of volume. Adding a fifth day, or extra sets, or a second supplemental template, because the program feels too easy in week two, is the fastest way to break a program that was working. If it is working, leave it alone.

Program-hopping. Six weeks of 5/3/1, then six weeks of Sheiko, then six weeks of Candito is not a year of training. It is three first cycles, and first cycles do not build totals. The lifters with the biggest totals are usually the lifters who ran the same program, or the same family of programs, for years. Boredom is not a reason to switch. A stall after two full cycles is.

Running the wrong template for the goal. BBB is for building size and work capacity. FSL is for building the lifts with sustainable volume. Running BBB when you need a peak, or running FSL when you need size, is not a program failure. It is a template mismatch, and the fix is to pick the template that matches the goal, not to blame the program. The goal decides the template more than the template decides the goal.

Putting it together

Start this week. Pick your four lifts, set your training maxes at 90 percent, and write down the weights for the first cycle: 65, 75, and 85 percent in week one, 70, 80, and 90 in week two, 75, 85, and 95 in week three, and the deload at 40, 50, and 60 in week four. Pick a template — BBB if you want size, FSL if you want sustainable volume — and run it as written for two full cycles. Write down every number: the top set weight, the AMRAP reps, and how the set actually felt. In eight weeks, compare the numbers, not the feeling, and adjust one thing if something is not moving. If the numbers moved, the program worked, and the next cycle starts from a stronger place, five and ten pounds heavier. If they did not, the answer is in the log: the training max, the template, or the recovery, and the fix is one change, not a new program.

5/3/1 has lasted since 2009 because it is honest about what a program can do. It cannot make you strong in six weeks. It can make you stronger, month after month, for years, if you run it as written and let the small increases compound. That is not a exciting promise. It is a true one, and a true promise kept for a year is worth more than an exciting promise broken in six weeks. The program is simple. That is the whole point. Run it, write it down, and let the bar tell you the truth.
