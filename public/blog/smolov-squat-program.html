← The Strength Blog PROGRAM GUIDE · SQUAT SPECIALIZATION One Lift. Thirteen Weeks.

Smolov is the most famous squat program ever written — and the most misunderstood. Thirteen weeks, four squat days a week at the start, one lift pushed harder than almost any other program dares. It has built enormous squats. It has also buried lifters who ran it at the wrong time, on the wrong base, with the wrong recovery. Here is the whole program, phase by phase, with the honest verdict on who should run it — and who should run something else.

Center Mass Strength

Every strength sport has a program with a reputation that outruns its instructions. For the squat, that program is Smolov. Mention it in any serious gym and someone will tell you a story: a hundred pounds on a squat in thirteen weeks, or a lifter who limped out of week three and never squatted heavy again. Both stories are true, which is exactly why Smolov deserves a careful read before anyone touches it. It is a Russian squat specialization and peaking cycle, credited to Sergey Smolov, built on a simple and brutal idea: squat a lot, squat heavy, squat often, and let the volume force adaptation that gentler programs never demand. Run at the right time, on a solid base, with recovery treated as seriously as the training, it works. Run as a shortcut, it punishes you. This guide lays out all four phases, the exact set-and-rep schemes, what happens to your other lifts, what the recovery bill actually looks like, and the over-40 verdict — because the lifter reading this site is not a 22-year-old with unlimited recovery, and the honest answer for most of us is not the full thirteen weeks.

The short version

Smolov is a 13-week squat specialization cycle. It has four phases: a 2-week introductory microcycle, a 3-week base mesocycle (plus a test week), a 2-week switching phase, and a 5-week intense mesocycle that ends with a new one-rep max test. The base mesocycle is the famous part: four squat sessions a week, Monday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday, with the same weekly shape — 4 sets of 9, 5 sets of 7, 7 sets of 5, and 10 sets of 3 — at 70, 75, 80, and 85 percent of your one-rep max, with the weights climbing week to week.

It is not a general strength program. During the full cycle, the squat is the program. Bench press and deadlift get parked or maintained at best, because heavy pulling on top of this squat volume is how lifters break down. Smolov is a peaking tool for a lifter with a specific reason to peak — a meet, a record attempt, a squat goal with a date on it — not a year-round way to train.

The gains are real and so is the cost. Lifters who finish the cycle commonly report 50 to 100 pounds on their squat, sometimes more for lifters whose squat was undertrained relative to their base strength. The cost is thirteen weeks of your training life, four squat days a week at the start, serious eating and sleeping demands, and a real risk of burnout or injury if your base, technique, or recovery is not ready. If any of those three is shaky, the smarter move is usually Smolov Jr. — the 3-week condensed version — or a submaximal squat program that builds the same base without the same bill.

What Smolov actually is

Smolov comes out of the Soviet weightlifting tradition, where specialization cycles were a normal part of an athlete's year: pick a lift, hammer it for a block, then move on. It was not designed as a beginner program, and it was never meant to be run back-to-back all year. The logic is high-frequency, high-volume overload. Most programs squat once or twice a week; Smolov squats four times a week in the base phase, and the sheer tonnage — sets times reps times weight — is far beyond what most lifters have ever handled. The body adapts to that tonnage in two ways: technique gets grooved to automaticity because you practice the lift constantly, and the muscles and connective tissue that drive the squat get forced to grow and strengthen under a load they cannot ignore.

That is also why the program is so unforgiving about percentages. Smolov's loads are calculated off a true, recent one-rep max — not a hopeful one, not a gym PR from three years ago, not a number you wish you squatted. The barbell squat guide covers how to test and set that number honestly, and the difference between a true max and an inflated one is the difference between a hard program and an impossible one. If your max is inflated by 20 pounds, every set in the base mesocycle is heavier than prescribed, and the error compounds across four sessions a week. Test first. Be honest. Round down if you are unsure.

One more thing Smolov is not: a technique-learning program. Four sessions a week of heavy squatting will groove whatever technique you bring to it, good or bad. If your squat has a major flaw — knees collapsing hard, a chest that folds on every heavy rep, depth that is a negotiation — Smolov will engrave it deeper under fatigue. Fix the big flaw first, with the kind of submaximal work in the powerlifting program guide, and come back to Smolov with a lift worth repeating ten thousand times.

Who Smolov is for — and who should skip it

Smolov suits an intermediate to advanced lifter with at least a couple of solid years of consistent squatting, a squat that has genuinely stalled on normal programming, and a calendar with thirteen clear weeks. You need to be able to train four days a week without negotiation, eat enough to recover, sleep enough to adapt, and handle a program that will make your legs sore in a way you have probably never experienced. You also need a reason: a meet date, a milestone, a squat goal that justifies parking everything else for a quarter of a year.

Skip the full cycle if you are a beginner, if you are coming back from injury, if your squat technique is still a work in progress, if you cannot commit to the schedule, or if you are in a calorie deficit. Smolov on a deficit is a stall wearing a program's clothes — the volume demands fuel, and under-eating turns a hard block into a breakdown. Skip it too if your bench or deadlift is the priority right now, because both will wait while the squat takes over. And skip it if you are already beat up: this program adds stress to a system, it does not heal one. The form guide for older lifters is the better starting point when the joints are the limiting factor, not the program.

The middle path, and the one most lifters should take first, is Smolov Jr. It is the base mesocycle's shape condensed into three weeks, it works for the squat or the bench press, and it tells you honestly whether you tolerate this kind of frequency and volume. If three weeks of Smolov Jr. leaves you stronger and hungry for more, the full cycle is a reasonable conversation. If it leaves you wrecked, you just learned that lesson in three weeks instead of thirteen.

Phase 1: the introductory microcycle (weeks 1–2)

The full cycle opens with two weeks that look almost gentle compared to what follows, and that is the point. The introductory microcycle builds work capacity and re-acclimates you to frequent squatting before the volume lands. Expect two to three squat sessions a week at moderate weights, in the 65 to 75 percent range, for sets of 6 to 8 — enough to groove the pattern and shake off rust, not enough to bury you. Lifters who skip this phase and jump straight into the base mesocycle usually regret it by the second week, because the base phase assumes a body already used to squatting often. Treat the intro as part of the program, not a warm-up you can cut. If you are coming off a normal program with regular squatting, this phase still earns its keep: it is the last easy fortnight you will see for a while.

Phase 2: the base mesocycle (weeks 3–6)

This is the phase everyone means when they say "Smolov." Three weeks of building volume, then a fourth week that backs off to a single squat session and a test of your new max. The weekly shape never changes — only the weights do:

Monday: 4 sets of 9 at 70 percent. The highest-rep day, and the one that teaches you what real squat conditioning feels like. Nine reps at 70 percent sounds reasonable until it is the fourth set and your legs are telling you otherwise. Rest generously between sets — this is not conditioning work, and rushing the rest turns a strength session into a metcon.

Wednesday: 5 sets of 7 at 75 percent. Heavier, fewer reps, still a lot of total work. By Wednesday the Monday soreness is a fact of life, and the skill being trained is squatting well while sore. That skill transfers: meet day rarely arrives with fresh legs.

Friday: 7 sets of 5 at 80 percent. The volume peak of the week in terms of heavy reps. Seven sets of five at 80 percent is a serious session on its own; as the third squat day of the week it is the day most lifters remember. Keep the technique identical from the first set to the last — the moment form breaks down under fatigue is the moment to take longer rests, not to grind ugly reps.

Saturday: 10 sets of 3 at 85 percent. Ten triples at 85 percent, the day after the heaviest volume day. This is the session that separates lifters who recover from lifters who merely survive. Triples keep each set short, but ten of them at 85 percent add up to thirty heavy reps, and the last few sets are where the program does its real work — and where ego does its real damage. If a set moves badly, the answer is rest, not a tenth set with compromised form.

How the weights climb: week two adds roughly 20 pounds to each day's load, and week three adds roughly 30 pounds relative to week one — so the same 4x9, 5x7, 7x5, 10x3 shape, just heavier. Some versions express the jumps in kilograms (about 10 kg, then about 15 kg); the principle is identical. Small, planned jumps, never a jump because a day felt easy. The program's progression is written; your job is to execute it, not to improve on it mid-cycle.

Week four is the test week. The volume drops away almost entirely — a single squat session, building to a heavy single, and a test of your new max. That new max becomes the basis for the intense mesocycle's percentages, which is why testing honestly matters twice over: an inflated test max makes the hardest phase of the program harder than written, and the intense phase is already the hardest part. If you miss reps during the three building weeks, the standard fix is to repeat the week at the same weights rather than pushing on into heavier loads you cannot complete. Missing reps is information, not failure — but ignoring it is how a hard program becomes an injury.

What about everything else? During the base mesocycle, accessory work stays minimal and squat-adjacent: light upper-body maintenance, core work, mobility, walking. Heavy deadlifts are out — they compete directly with the squat volume for the same back, hips, and recovery budget, and lifters who try to keep both usually lose both. Bench press can be maintained with a couple of light sessions, but chasing a bench PR during Smolov is a second program wearing a maintenance costume. The Russian squat program guide is the natural comparison here: it is a gentler, longer squat specialization (three days a week, a slower build) and the right call when Smolov's four-day volume is more than your life can hold.

Phase 3: the switching phase (weeks 7–8)

After the base mesocycle, Smolov gives you two weeks that look like a break and are actually a bridge. The switching phase is active recovery with a purpose: light, explosive work — box jumps, jump squats, power cleans, dynamic-effort squats at around 50 percent for fast triples — two or three times a week, plus stretching and easy movement on the other days. No heavy squatting. The point is to let the fatigue of the base phase dissipate while keeping the nervous system sharp and the squat pattern alive, so you arrive at the intense mesocycle recovered rather than merely rested. Lifters who skip the switching phase and jump straight into heavy work carry base-phase fatigue into the hardest block, and the intense phase punishes that. Two weeks feels long when you are impatient to test your new strength; it is short compared to the cost of starting the intense phase tired. Use the time: sleep, eat, stretch, walk, and let the soreness fully leave before the real peaking starts.

Phase 4: the intense mesocycle (weeks 9–13)

The intense mesocycle is five weeks of heavy, lower-volume squatting, three days a week, with much of the work between 80 and 95 percent of the max you tested after the base phase. The rep schemes shift with the week — sets of 5, 4, and 3 at climbing percentages — and the total weekly volume is lower than the base phase, but the intensity is higher, which is a different kind of hard. Where the base phase asked "can you do a lot of squatting," the intense phase asks "can you do heavy squatting, well, three times a week, for a month." Week five of the block tapers: a single session of 4 sets of 4 at around 85 percent, then three full days of rest, then the test — your new one-rep max, the number the whole thirteen weeks were pointed at.

The intense phase is where most lifters who quit, quit — not because the weights are impossible, but because the accumulated fatigue of thirteen weeks meets the heaviest loads of the program. The management rules are the same ones that carried you through the base phase, just more important: sleep eight hours, eat enough, take the rest days as rest days, and do not add volume. Nothing you add in week eleven helps week thirteen; everything you add risks it. If a session goes badly, finish what you can with good form and move on — one bad session in a peaking block is noise, not a verdict, and chasing it with extra work is how a taper gets ruined.

After the test, do not run it again immediately. Smolov ends with a max test, and the correct next step is a lighter block — submaximal volume, general strength work, rebuilding the bench and deadlift you parked — before any thought of another specialization cycle. The powerlifting exercises guide is a good map for that rebuilding phase: the accessories that support all three lifts, chosen by weakness, rather than another thirteen weeks of one lift. A specialization cycle earns its keep once or twice a year at most; run year-round, it stops being specialization and starts being a rut.

Smolov Jr.: the 3-week version most lifters should start with

Smolov Jr. takes the base mesocycle's shape and runs it for three weeks, four days a week, and it works for the squat or the bench press. The weekly shape is 6 sets of 6 at 70 percent, 7 sets of 5 at 75 percent, 8 sets of 4 at 80 percent, and 10 sets of 3 at 85 percent, with the weights climbing by roughly 5 to 10 pounds in week two and 10 to 20 pounds in week three. That is a meaningful jump in three weeks — commonly 20 to 40 pounds on the lift — and it fits inside a normal training life in a way the full cycle does not. You can run Jr. twice in a row with a week of rest between cycles, and many lifters do, but even Jr. deserves respect: four days a week of the same lift at those percentages is still a specialization block, and your other lifts still take a back seat while it runs. The difference is the size of the commitment, and for most lifters — especially lifters over 40 — that difference is the whole decision.

Recovery: the part of the program that is not sets and reps

Smolov's programming is fixed, which means everything you actually control sits outside the gym. The lifters who finish Smolov strong are rarely the ones who tried hardest in the gym; they are the ones who recovered hardest outside it. Sleep is first. Eight hours is the floor during this program, not the aspiration — adaptation happens in sleep, and four squat days a week on six hours is a debt that compounds weekly. Food is second. You need to eat to recover from this volume; lifters who run Smolov in a deficit stall, and lifters who eat at maintenance often find that is not enough either. Protein high, calories at least at maintenance and usually above, and no heroic dieting for thirteen weeks. Stress is third and most ignored. A brutal work stretch, a bad sleep week, a life crisis — all of it draws from the same recovery budget as the squat volume, and the program does not adjust for it. If life gets genuinely hard mid-cycle, the right move is to repeat a week or step back to Jr., not to white-knuckle through loads your recovery cannot support.

Mobility and soft-tissue work earn their place here too, not as a warm-up ritual but as maintenance on a machine doing four times its normal squat mileage: hips, ankles, thoracic spine, and the tissues around the knees, daily, in small doses. And a training partner helps more here than in almost any other program — not for spotting, though that matters at these loads, but for the simple fact that week ten at 8 a.m. on a Saturday is a much easier session with someone else in the room. Smolov is a lonely program by design; do not make it lonelier than it has to be.

Common mistakes that sink a Smolov run

Starting with an inflated max. The single most common error, and the one that poisons every week that follows. Test honestly, round down, and remember that a slightly easy first week is a feature — the program gets hard on its own schedule.

Keeping heavy deadlifts. The deadlift and the squat draw on the same recovery, and Smolov already spends all of it. Park the heavy pulls. They will be there in thirteen weeks, and they usually come back quickly once the squat volume drops.

Adding volume. Extra accessories, extra sessions, extra "just a few sets" work — all of it is load the program did not prescribe, on a body already at its prescribed limit. If you have energy left after a Smolov session, you are either early in the cycle or not resting long enough between sets. Save it.

Skipping the switching phase. Two weeks of light explosive work feels like wasted time to an impatient lifter. It is the reason the intense phase is survivable. Do not cut it.

Running it back-to-back. Smolov is a peaking cycle, not a lifestyle. Finish, test, rebuild with general strength work, and let at least a full training block pass before considering another specialization run.

Ignoring pain that is not soreness. Smolov soreness is deep, constant, and normal. Sharp pain, pain that changes your technique, pain that gets worse across a week — that is not soreness, and grinding through it is not toughness. Back off, get it looked at, and remember that a skipped week costs you a week while a serious injury costs you a season.

The over-40 verdict

Here is the part that matters most for this audience, said plainly. The full thirteen-week Smolov cycle is a young lifter's program in its demands, even though older lifters can absolutely run it with the right base. The volume is the issue, not the weights: four squat days a week for three weeks, then five weeks of heavy triples, is a recovery bill that gets harder to pay as sleep gets lighter, joints get creakier, and life gets fuller. That does not make it impossible after 40 — lifters in their forties and fifties have finished Smolov and set PRs — but it raises the entry requirements. Before you commit, you should already be squatting three times a week comfortably, sleeping well, eating enough, and free of any knee, hip, or back issue that heavy frequency would aggravate. If any of those is not true, the honest answer is not to force it.

For most lifters over 40, the right Smolov is Smolov Jr. Three weeks, same shape, a real jump, and a recovery bill you can actually pay. Run it, test, take a week easy, and decide from evidence whether a longer specialization block makes sense. And if even Jr. feels like too much frequency, that is useful information too — it points to the program this site is actually built around: submaximal squatting at honest percentages, progressed week to week, with the volume your recovery supports rather than the volume a famous program demands. The powerlifting program guide is that program, and for a lifter who wants to still be squatting heavy at 50, it beats a heroic thirteen weeks followed by six months of aching knees. The best squat program is not the hardest one you survived. It is the one that leaves you stronger a year from now — and for most of us, that program is the patient one.

None of that is an argument against ambition. If you have the base, the recovery, the calendar, and a date that justifies thirteen weeks of one lift, Smolov is a legitimate, time-tested way to chase a big squat PR, and this guide gives you the whole map. Just go in with your eyes open: test honestly, eat and sleep like it is part of the program (because it is), park the deadlift, do not skip the switching phase, and respect the difference between soreness and pain. Do that, and whatever number you test at the end of week thirteen will be a number you earned — not one you survived.
