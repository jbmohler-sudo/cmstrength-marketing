Barbell Squat, Done Right.

The squat is the most technical of the big three — the bar rides your back and every error in the setup shows up at the bottom; here is the setup, high bar vs. low bar compared honestly, the mistakes everyone makes, and programming that builds a lifetime squat.

Center Mass Strength

Why the Squat Is the Way It Is

The squat is the only lift where the bar rides on you. The bench presses the bar away from your chest. The deadlift picks the bar up off the floor. The squat straps a loaded barbell to your back and asks you to go down and come back up while keeping the whole system's center of mass over a four-inch patch of mid-foot. Everything about squat technique — the stance, the torso angle, the brace — is downstream of that one fact: it is a balance problem wearing a strength costume.

That's why it's the most technical of the big three, and why it's first in every powerlifting meet. The squat sets the tone for the whole day: it's the heaviest demand on the most muscle mass, it has the strictest geometric standard (hip crease below the top of the knee, judged by humans in real time), and it cannot be faked. You can't hitch a squat. You can't bounce a squat past depth and call it good. The rep either meets the standard or it doesn't, and everyone in the room sees it.

The good news: because the squat is a positions lift, it's also the most fixable lift. A deadlift fix might take months of rebuilding a setup. A squat fix is often one cue — bar an inch lower, stance an inch wider, brace actually on — and twenty pounds appears. This guide is the whole thing: why the bar position decides the torso angle, high bar vs. low bar with no tribalism, the rep from unrack to rerack, the mistakes everyone makes, programming that builds it for decades, and what changes after 40.

The bench is a push. The deadlift is a pull. The squat is a balance problem — and the setup decides the balance.

What this guide covers: why bar position decides torso angle (and why the bar must stay over mid-foot either way), high bar vs. low bar honestly compared, the rep from unrack to rerack, the universal mistakes with mechanical fixes, programming (frequency, rep ranges, RPE ceilings, variations matched to failure points), and squats after 40. High Bar vs. Low Bar, Honestly

Most of the internet treats this like a religion. It's a geometry problem. The bar sits in one of two places; the torso angle adjusts so the bar stays over mid-foot; everything else follows. Neither position is morally superior. Your build, your shoulder health, and your goals decide — and the honest test is a training block of each, not a comment-section argument.

High BarLow Bar
Bar sits on the trap shelf, on top of the shouldersBar sits on the rear-delt shelf, an inch or two lower
More upright torso — the bar is closer to the hips verticallyMore forward lean — the torso tips so the lower bar stays over mid-foot
Knees travel farther forward; ankles need more dorsiflexionKnees stay back; hips travel farther back
Quad-dominant — the upright torso puts the knees to workPosterior-chain dominant — glutes, hamstrings, and back share more of the load

The practical read: if you compete in powerlifting, low bar is the default for a reason — it puts more weight on the bar, and the sport measures weight on the bar. Give it an honest 8–12 week block before judging it, because the first month of low bar feels like learning to squat again and everyone quits in week three. If your shoulders won't tolerate it — and plenty of masters lifters' shoulders won't — high bar with committed quad work is a completely legitimate squat. The total doesn't care about your bar position.

One law governs both: the bar stays over mid-foot. Torso angle differs because bar position differs, but the balance point doesn't move. A low-bar squat with the weight on the toes is not a low-bar squat — it's a mistake wearing the right bar position. Film from the side, watch the bar path, and you'll see the truth in one set. If your shoulders fight the low-bar position, the mobility and setup work in Low Bar Squat With Stiff Shoulders is the fix before you abandon the position.

The Rep, From Unrack to Rerack

Do this identically on every rep, light or heavy. The squat pattern your body learns on the warmups is the pattern that shows up under a max — and the only way the max looks like the warmup is if there is no difference between them. Sloppy light reps are rehearsal for sloppy heavy reps.

  • Set the bar height right. The bar should sit at about sternum to nipple height — low enough that you slightly bend your knees to get under it, high enough that you unrack by straightening your legs. If you're calf-raising the bar out of the hooks, it's too high, and you'll be walking out heavy weight on your toes. Fix it before the first rep, not after the third set.
  • Grip as narrow as your shoulders allow. Narrower hands pull the upper back tighter and build a thicker shelf for the bar. But "as narrow as your shoulders allow" is the whole sentence — if the elbows and shoulders ache, go wider. Wrists stay straight with the bar in the heel of the hand, not draped over bent-back wrists. The hands wedge the bar into the back; they don't hold it up.
  • Duck under and place the bar. High bar: on the trap shelf, sitting in the meat, not on the neck bones. Low bar: on the rear-delt shelf, pulled in tight with the lats. Either way, drive your elbows under the bar — not flared behind you — to lock the shelf. The bar should feel like it's part of your back before it ever leaves the hooks.
  • Brace before you unrack. Big breath into the belly, ribs pulled down, trunk locked like you're about to take a punch. You unrack with the brace already on — the bar doesn't get to leave the hooks while you're soft.
  • Stand up with it. Three steps back. Unrack by straightening the knees, not by squatting the weight up. Then the walkout: one foot back, second foot back, one small step to even up. That's it. No dance, no five-step wander, no adjusting for ten seconds. Every step past three is balance and energy you'll need at the bottom.
  • Set the feet, check the balance. Stance roughly shoulder-width to a bit wider, toes out 15–30 degrees. Before you descend, feel the weight across the whole foot — not the toes, not the heels, the middle. If you're already on your toes standing still, the rep is broken before it starts.
  • Re-brace at the top. Belly breath, ribs down, trunk locked. The brace you set standing is the brace you keep all the way to the hole. A squat without a brace is a wish.
  • Break at the hips and knees together. Not hips first — that's a good-morning squat. Not knees first — that's a knee slide with the hips chasing. Hips and knees unlock at the same time, and the torso sets its angle in the first six inches and holds it.
  • Descend under control. Two to three seconds down. Knees track over the toes — shove them out, don't let them cave. Chest stays in the slot it set at the top. Hit depth: hip crease clearly below the top of the knee. "Clearly" matters — a rep that needs slow-motion review is a rep the judges will red-light.
  • Turn it around tight. Use the stretch reflex — a tight, fast reversal at the bottom, not a pause and not a dive-bomb bounce. You're rebounding off tension, not relaxing into the hole and hoping.
  • Drive the bar straight up. Push the floor away and think about keeping your chest in position — hips and chest rise together. The bar path is a straight vertical line over mid-foot. If your hips shoot up and your chest dumps forward, that's not a sticking point, that's the quad strength not matching the back strength, and it's programmed away (see below).
  • Rerack by walking straight in. Walk forward until the bar touches the uprights, then set it down into the hooks. Never try to rack by feel from a step away — that's how bars get dropped and spotters get stories.

Film your heavy sets from the side, bar height visible, the whole rep in frame. The checklist above is a felt sense; the video is the measurement. Most lifters who think they hit depth are two inches high, and most lifters who think the bar path is vertical are drifting forward. The camera doesn't argue.

The Mistakes Everyone Makes

Same mistakes, same gyms, same order of frequency. The fixes are mechanical, not motivational:

  • Knees cave in under load. Valgus collapse — the knees dive toward each other coming out of the hole. Fix: screw your feet into the floor and shove the knees out over the toes from the first inch of the descent. If it only happens heavy, the glutes aren't strong enough to hold the groove at that load — drop the weight until the knees track clean, then build back with paused squats, which teach the bottom position under control. Also check stance width: too wide for your hip structure is a common cause of cave.
  • The good-morning squat. Chest dumps forward out of the hole, hips shoot up, and the rep finishes as a back extension wearing a squat costume. Fix: this is almost always quads too weak relative to the back — the body shifts the load to the stronger muscles. The prescription is front squats and high-bar work to build the quads (see the front squat guide), plus the cue "chest up" out of the hole. Drop the weight until hips and chest rise together.
  • Butt wink — the lower back rounds at the bottom. Going deeper than the hips allow. Fix: depth stops where the back starts moving. Widen the stance a touch, point the toes out a bit more, and stop an inch above where the pelvis tucks under. Box squats to a box set at legal depth retrain the endpoint beautifully. And some perspective: a little pelvic tilt at rock bottom isn't a spinal emergency — but a back that rounds and then re-extends under a loaded barbell is a real problem. Film from the side and find your honest depth.
  • Weight drifts onto the toes. The bar path bends forward, the heels get light, and the rep becomes a fight against tipping. Fix: whole-foot pressure — think about pushing through the middle of the foot, not the balls. Check bar position first: a bar sitting too high on a low-bar squatter's back will roll forward all set long. And check the descent: diving the last two inches throws the weight forward every time.
  • The brace leaks. Breathing into the chest, ribs flared, trunk soft — then wondering why the bottom feels like a collapsing tent. Fix: breathe into the belly at the top, ribs down, hold it through the whole rep (the Valsalva), exhale at the top. One breath per rep on anything heavy. The belt helps the brace, but the brace comes first — a belt on a soft trunk is a fashion choice.
  • Cutting depth — or dive-bombing it. Either stopping high to protect the ego or dropping uncontrolled to steal a bounce. Fix: tempo squats (three seconds down, two-count pause, drive up) teach position under control, and a box set to legal depth calibrates the endpoint. Depth is not a feeling. It's a measurement, and the side camera is the ruler.

Notice the pattern: every one of these is a positions problem or a strength-balance problem. Nobody's squat is limited by a lack of effort. They're limited by a setup built in a hurry or by quads that never got their own training block.

How to Program It

The squat is the volume lift. It responds to frequency, to submaximal reps done with perfect positions, and to patience — and it punishes ego faster than any other lift, because a squat grinder is a spine loading event. Program it like the long game it is.

Frequency. Two squat days a week is the standard for intermediates: one heavy, one light-to-moderate. Early intermediates can run three (heavy, light, moderate) and progress weekly; most lifters past that stage do better with two quality days than three beat-up ones. Masters lifters often land on one heavy day plus one technique or variation day — the pattern stays greased without the recovery bill of two heavy sessions.

Rep ranges. The squat lives in the 1–5 range for strength work, with 3s and 5s doing the heavy lifting of most programs. Sets of 6–8 belong on the secondary day for hypertrophy and work capacity. Singles are for testing and peaking, not training — a steady diet of heavy singles builds a great single and a stalled squat. The set of 5 at RPE 8 is the workhorse of squat programming and has been for decades, because it delivers the reps that build strength without the grinders that cost it.

RPE ceilings. Cap the heavy day's top sets at RPE 8–9. The squat's last rep is the most expensive rep in the gym — form breaks down exactly when the load is highest, and that's where backs get "tweaked." Leave the RPE 10s for planned test days and the platform. If the periodization guide's block structure is new to you, the short version: the squat gets built with volume in the accumulation block, sharpened with intensity in the strength block, and maintained — not built — in the peak.

Variations, matched to the failure point. Don't guess. Film the miss, find where the bar stops, and pick the variation with that sticking point's name on it:

  • Miss out of the hole → paused squats (2-count pause at the bottom) and pin squats set at the hole. These build starting strength where the stretch reflex can't help.
  • Sticking point just above parallel → tempo squats (slow descent, no bounce) and pin squats set at the sticking point. These build position and strength through the transition.
  • Chest dumps forward / upper back collapses → front squats and safety-bar squats. Nothing punishes a soft upper back like a front squat — and front squats build the same prime movers with less spinal loading; Gullett et al. (2009) found front squats produce comparable muscle activation to back squats with lower compressive forces (PubMed 19002072).
  • Weak lockout / glutes don't finish → box squats (sit back, drive hips through) and good mornings done light and strict. The lockout is a hip-extension problem wearing a squat costume.
  • Depth is inconsistent → box squats to a box at legal depth, every rep touching. The box is a teacher, not a crutch — touch, don't collapse onto it.

Volume. Most intermediates build on 10–20 hard sets a week across the two days. Start at the low end, add a set per week until progress stalls or recovery complains, then hold. More is not better — recoverable is better. The squat is the lift where junk volume shows up first, as achy knees and a dead-feeling lower back.

Squats After 40

The squat after 40 is the same lift with a different recovery clock and a lower tolerance for slop. The positions don't change — bar over mid-foot, knees tracking, depth legal — but everything around the positions gets more deliberate, because a 45-year-old's knees and back keep score longer than a 25-year-old's.

Three practical adjustments. First, the warmup gets real. Ten to fifteen minutes now, not three. Hips and ankles get first attention — bodyweight squats, goblet squats, ankle mobility — and the bar doesn't get loaded until the empty-bar reps look like the work sets. The masters lifters who stay healthy are the ones whose warmup is a ritual, not a suggestion.

Second, find your honest depth and defend it. Hip anatomy varies, and it varies more with mileage. If rock-bottom depth breaks your back into a butt wink, your legal depth is where your back stays flat — usually a slightly wider stance with the toes out a touch more. The competition standard doesn't change, but training depth should respect the spine you have. A 2010 review of squat kinematics by Schoenfeld found no evidence that deep squats performed with proper technique inherently increase knee injury risk (PubMed 20182386) — the risk isn't the depth, it's the depth your structure can't hold.

Third, the variations earn their keep. Box squats for depth honesty. The safety-bar squat for shoulders and elbows that veto low bar. Tempo squats for joint-friendly intensity — slow reps at moderate weight build the positions without the joint pounding of max-effort grinding. And single-leg work — split squats, lunges, step-ups — is cheap insurance for knee health and balance that most lifters skip until something hurts.

On programming: one heavy day plus one light or technique day is plenty. Cap top sets at RPE 8 most of the year — the grinder rep costs a masters lifter more and teaches less. And belt up earlier in the warmup ramp without guilt; the belt is a tool, not a confession. Barbell Squat: Common Questions

Should I squat high bar or low bar?

If you compete in powerlifting, low bar — it puts more weight on the bar through a shorter lever arm, and the sport measures weight. If your shoulders won't tolerate the position, or you train for general strength and quad development, high bar is a completely legitimate squat. Either way the law is the same: bar over mid-foot, knees tracking over toes, hip crease below the knee. Give each an honest 8–12 week block before deciding; the first month of a new bar position always feels wrong.

How deep do I need to squat?

For powerlifting, the standard is hip crease below the top of the knee — clearly below, not arguably below. For general training, the same depth is the target: it's the depth that loads the glutes and quads fully. Don't chase depth your structure can't hold — depth stops where your back stays flat. A 2010 review of squat kinematics found no evidence that deep squats with proper technique inherently increase knee injury risk; the danger is depth plus a collapsing back, not depth itself.

Why do my knees cave in when it gets heavy?

Because your glutes can't hold the groove at that load. The knees are supposed to track over the toes; when they dive inward, the adductors are overpowering the external rotators. Screw your feet into the floor, shove the knees out from the first inch, and drop the weight until the knees track clean. Paused squats rebuild the bottom position, and checking stance width matters — too wide for your hips is a common cause.

What's "butt wink" and do I need to fix it?

Butt wink is the pelvis tucking under — the lower back rounding — at the very bottom of the squat. A little of it at rock bottom isn't a spinal emergency, but a back that rounds and re-extends under load is a real problem. The fix is mechanical: depth stops where the back starts moving. Widen the stance slightly, turn the toes out a bit more, and use box squats at legal depth to retrain the endpoint.

When should I start using a belt for squats?

When your brace is the limiter rather than your technique — typically once sets above about 80 percent of your max are a regular thing. A belt doesn't protect your back by itself; it gives your braced trunk something to push against so you can create more intra-abdominal pressure. Learn to brace hard without it first, then add the belt on your heavy sets. Masters lifters: belting up earlier in the warmup ramp is fine.

How do I breathe during a heavy squat?

Belly breath at the top, ribs down, hold it through the whole rep, exhale at the top. That's the Valsalva — it pressurizes the trunk and turns your midsection into a rigid column. One breath per rep on anything heavy. Breathing into the chest, exhaling on the way down, or taking a second breath at the bottom all leak the brace exactly when the load is highest.
