← The Strength Blog SQUAT STRENGTH STANDARDS · BY BODYWEIGHT How Strong Is Strong? Find Your Level.

A 315-pound squat is a lifetime achievement for one lifter and a warm-up for another. The difference is bodyweight, training age, and what you compare against. These are the squat strength standards by bodyweight — and the honest path from one level to the next.

Center Mass Strength

Every lifter has done the same quiet math in the gym: is my squat actually good? The bar does not answer that question. Three hundred pounds is a number, and a number without a denominator is just a weight on a bar. A 315-pound squat at a 150-pound bodyweight is more than double bodyweight — an advanced lift by any standard. The same 315 at a 250-pound bodyweight is barely more than bodyweight, and it is the kind of squat a big lifter hits in their first year. The standard is not the weight. The standard is the weight relative to the person lifting it, and the standards in this guide are built on that ratio: your one-rep max divided by your bodyweight, measured against the same five levels every strength database uses, from beginner to elite. Find your ratio, find your level, and then the useful question becomes the one this guide is really about: what does it take to climb to the next one?

Standards are not records, and they are not pass-fail lines. They are population benchmarks — compiled from millions of logged lifts in databases like Strength Level, which draws on more than 25 million recorded squats — and they describe where a lifter sits relative to other lifters at the same bodyweight. The levels are roughly percentiles: beginner is around the 5th percentile, novice the 20th, intermediate the 50th, advanced the 80th, and elite the 95th. That framing matters, because it tells you what a level actually means. Intermediate does not mean average in the sense of mediocre. It means you squat more than half of everyone who trains with a barbell at your bodyweight, which puts you ahead of almost everyone who has ever walked into a gym. And elite does not mean competitive. It means you are in the top few percent of consistent lifters — strong enough that strangers notice, not necessarily strong enough to win a meet.

The short version

Judge your squat by the ratio, not the weight. Divide your one-rep max by your bodyweight. For men, the widely used bands are roughly 0.75 times bodyweight for beginner, 1.25 for novice, 1.5 for intermediate, 2.25 for advanced, and 2.75 for elite. For women, the bands run about 0.5, 0.75, 1.25, 1.5, and 2.0 times bodyweight. Use a true one-rep max, at parallel depth. The standards assume a raw back squat to competition depth — hip crease below the top of the knee — with no more than a belt. A high squat, a set of five, or a working weight compared against a 1RM table will all make you look weaker than you are, or stronger, depending on which direction you fudge. Each level takes longer than the last. Beginner to novice is a matter of months. Novice to intermediate is one to two years of consistent training. Intermediate to advanced is two to five years. Advanced to elite is five-plus years and usually a level of commitment that looks less like a hobby and more like a sport.

The standards are a map, not a verdict. Limb length, training age, age itself, and bodyweight all move where you land, and the same ratio gets harder to hold as bodyweight climbs — strength grows more slowly than body mass. Use the level to pick the next goal, not to decide whether you belong in the gym.

How strength standards work

Every strength standard is built on the same idea: express the lift as a multiple of bodyweight, then sort lifters into bands. The reason is simple. Absolute weight favors the heavy. A 220-pound lifter squats more than a 150-pound lifter with the same training, the same effort, and the same genetics, because there is more muscle behind the bar. Bodyweight ratios correct for that, and they are the reason a 300-pound squat can be elite for a light lifter and ordinary for a heavy one. The correction is not perfect — heavier lifters need a slightly lower ratio to reach each level, because muscle mass does not scale one-to-one with body mass — but it is far more honest than comparing raw numbers across a gym full of people who weigh different amounts.

The five levels have rough training-age meanings attached to them, and those meanings are more useful than the numbers themselves. Beginner means you have been squatting for a few weeks to a few months: the movement is still new, and the weight goes up almost every session. Novice means three to nine months of regular training: you have learned the movement, and you are still adding weight most weeks. Intermediate means nine months to two years: progress has slowed to monthly, and you need a real program to keep moving. Advanced means two to five years: you are programming deliberately, managing recovery, and adding weight in small jumps across a training cycle. Elite means five-plus years of focused training, and it usually means competing, because almost nobody reaches the 95th percentile by accident. If your ratio says intermediate but your training age says six months, the ratio is probably inflated by a short heavy build, or a high squat, or a max that was not really a max. The levels and the timelines should roughly agree. When they do not, the measurement is the suspect, not the lifter.

One more thing the standards assume, and it is the thing lifters skip most often: depth. The numbers in this guide assume a back squat to parallel — the hip crease dropping below the top of the knee — which is the same standard a powerlifting judge applies. A squat cut an inch high is a different lift, and comparing it against a parallel-depth table is comparing two different movements. If you are not sure about your depth, the complete guide to powerlifting squat depth and hitting competition depth shows how to test it with video from the side, and the answer is usually more honest than the feeling in the bottom of the squat. The complete guide to prepping for your first powerlifting meet makes the same point from the other direction: the lift only counts if it would pass on a platform, and the standards only mean something if your squat would too.

Men's squat strength standards

The table below gives squat one-rep max standards for men at common bodyweights, in pounds. The ratios are the widely published bands — beginner 0.75 times bodyweight, novice 1.25, intermediate 1.5, advanced 2.25, elite 2.75 — rounded to the nearest five pounds. These are approximate benchmarks compiled from logged-lift databases, not federation records, and different sources disagree by five to ten percent on any given cell. Treat the table as a map with contour lines, not a set of legal limits.

BodyweightBeginner (0.75x)Novice (1.25x)Intermediate (1.5x)Advanced (2.25x)Elite (2.75x)
150 lb115 lb190 lb225 lb340 lb415 lb
180 lb135 lb225 lb270 lb405 lb495 lb
200 lb150 lb250 lb300 lb450 lb550 lb
220 lb165 lb275 lb330 lb495 lb605 lb

Read the table by your row, not by the column you wish you were in. A 180-pound lifter squatting 270 is intermediate, and that is a genuinely strong squat — half of everyone who trains at that bodyweight cannot do it. The same lifter at 405 is advanced, and at 495 is elite, which means they are squatting more than 95 percent of lifters their size. The jumps between levels are not equal. Beginner to novice is 90 pounds at 180 bodyweight. Intermediate to advanced is 135. Advanced to elite is another 90, and it is the hardest 90 pounds in the table, because it is gained after years of training, when every additional pound costs more than the last. That is the shape of strength: fast early, slow late, and the standards make the shape visible.

Notice what the table says about the famous milestones. A 315-pound squat — three plates — is intermediate for a 200-pound lifter and advanced for a 150-pound lifter. A 405-pound squat — four plates — is advanced at 180 and intermediate at 220. The plates on the bar are the same. The lifter is different. That is why the milestone that matters is not the plates but the ratio, and why the lifters who chase plate milestones without watching their bodyweight are chasing a number that moves every time they gain or lose ten pounds. The complete guide to powerlifting weight classes and picking yours makes this point about competition, and it applies in the gym too: the class you fill out is usually the class where your ratio is best, not the class where the plates look biggest.

Women's squat strength standards

Women's standards use the same structure and lower ratios — beginner 0.5 times bodyweight, novice 0.75, intermediate 1.25, advanced 1.5, elite 2.0 — rounded to the nearest five pounds. The gap between men's and women's ratios is mostly a matter of average muscle mass distribution, not effort or training quality, and it narrows with training age: the women who reach advanced and elite have usually been training as long and as seriously as the men at the same level.

BodyweightBeginner (0.5x)Novice (0.75x)Intermediate (1.25x)Advanced (1.5x)Elite (2.0x)
125 lb65 lb95 lb155 lb190 lb250 lb
150 lb75 lb115 lb190 lb225 lb300 lb
175 lb90 lb130 lb220 lb265 lb350 lb

A 150-pound woman squatting 190 is intermediate, and a 225 squat at that bodyweight is advanced — a 1.5-times-bodyweight squat that most men never reach. A 300-pound squat at 150 is elite, double bodyweight, and it is the kind of lift that turns heads in any gym in the country. The milestones land differently here too: two plates — 225 pounds — is advanced for a 150-pound woman and intermediate for a 175-pound woman, and three plates is elite at either bodyweight. The numbers are smaller than the men's table, and the achievement is not. A double-bodyweight squat is a double-bodyweight squat, and it is rare at any bodyweight, for either sex.

Women's strength standards are also where the depth assumption matters most, because the quarter-squat is more common among women who were never taught to squat to parallel, and a quarter-squat compared against a parallel table inflates the result by a lot. If your numbers put you at advanced but your training age is under a year, check the depth before you celebrate. The standard is only useful when the lift being measured is the lift the standard was built on.

How to test your squat honestly

Standards are only as honest as the max you feed them, and the max is where most lifters fudge, usually without meaning to. The first rule is to use a one-rep max, not a rep max and not a working weight. The tables are built around the heaviest load you can lift for a single clean repetition, and comparing a hard set of five against them makes you look weaker than you are — a five-rep max is roughly 85 to 87 percent of a one-rep max, so a lifter whose best set is 5 reps at 225 has a true max near 260, and the table should be read at 260. If you do not want to test a true max, estimate it from a rep max with a calculator, and use the estimate honestly. The 1RM calculator will do the math, but it cannot fix a rep max that was cut high or a depth that was never there.

The second rule is to test the max the way the standard defines it: a raw back squat, parallel depth, no more than a belt. Knee wraps add pounds. A squat suit adds more. A high squat adds the most of all, and it is the addition lifters are least honest about, because the difference between parallel and an inch high is invisible from the front and obvious from the side. Film the test from the side at hip height and judge the lowest frame. The complete barbell squat form and programming guide covers the setup that keeps a max attempt honest — the brace, the walkout, and the descent speed — and the max you test with that setup is the max the standards were built to measure.

The third rule is to test when you are fresh, not at the end of a hard week. A max tested after four heavy sessions is not your max. It is your tired max, and the standards will read it as a lower level than the one you actually occupy. Test after a deload, or at the end of a peak, when the body has had a chance to show what the training built. And test the same way every time — same bar, same depth standard, same conditions — so that the level you read this year is comparable to the level you read next year. A standard is a measuring stick, and a measuring stick that changes length between measurements is not measuring anything.

What each level actually takes

Beginner to novice is the fastest jump in the sport, and it is mostly a matter of showing up. A new lifter squatting two or three times a week, adding five pounds a session, goes from a 0.75-times-bodyweight squat to a 1.25-times-bodyweight squat in three to nine months, because the gains are mostly neurological: the body learns the movement, the muscles learn to fire together, and the weight climbs while the muscle barely changes. The training is simple — a linear progression, the same movement, the same schedule — and the lifters who stall here are almost always the ones who change the program before the program has had a chance to work. The beginner powerlifting program guide lays out that progression, and the honest advice is to run it until it stops working, which takes longer than most lifters think.

Novice to intermediate is where a program becomes necessary. Linear progression stops working somewhere around a 1.25-times-bodyweight squat, because the body can no longer recover from adding weight every session, and the lifter who keeps trying to add five pounds a workout starts missing reps and then starts dreading the gym. The fix is weekly progression instead of daily: the weight goes up once a week, the volume is managed across the week, and the squat is trained two or three times with different intensities. This is also the level where the weak points start to show — a lifter whose squat fails at the bottom needs pause work and leg drive, a lifter whose squat fails at the top needs lockout work — and the accessory choices start to matter. The jump takes nine months to two years, and it is the level where most lifters either get serious about programming or plateau for years.

Intermediate to advanced is the long climb, and it is where most lifters spend most of their training lives. A 1.5-times-bodyweight squat to a 2.25-times-bodyweight squat is two to five years of deliberate work, and the work changes character: the program is built in cycles, the volume and intensity are managed across months, and the lifter is making decisions about peaking, deloading, and weak-point training that a novice never has to make. This is also the level where recovery becomes the limiter more often than effort — sleep, food, and stress management start to matter as much as the program, because the weights are heavy enough that a bad week of recovery costs a training cycle. The lifters who reach advanced are not the ones who trained hardest. They are the ones who trained most consistently, through the years when the gains were slow and the program was boring.

Advanced to elite is a different sport. A 2.75-times-bodyweight squat for a man, or a 2-times-bodyweight squat for a woman, takes five-plus years of focused training, and it usually takes competing, because the structure of a meet — the peak, the taper, the platform — is what pulls the last pounds out of a lifter who has already been training for years. The lifters at this level are managing everything: bodyweight, sleep, nutrition, technique, and the long arc of a training year. They are also the lifters most likely to be limited by something they cannot change — limb length, hip structure, or age — and the ones who have learned to work around it. Elite is not a goal for everyone, and it does not need to be. Intermediate is already strong. Advanced is already very strong. Elite is the level where strength stops being a hobby and starts being a pursuit, and the standards are honest about that.

Age, bodyweight, and the fine print

The standards in this guide are for adult lifters, and age moves them. A 50-year-old lifter at intermediate is not a weaker version of a 25-year-old at intermediate — they are a lifter holding a high level of strength against a body that recovers more slowly and carries more wear. The standards do not adjust for age, which is a flaw, and it is a flaw that matters most to the lifters this site is built for. A masters lifter who reads their ratio and sees intermediate should know that the number understates the achievement, because the population the standards are drawn from skews young. How to powerlift after 40 covers what changes with age — recovery, joint management, and the programming that keeps a lifter strong for decades — and the message is that the standard to chase is the one that fits your decade, not someone else's.

Bodyweight moves the standards too, in both directions. A lifter who gains twenty pounds of bodyweight and adds ten pounds to their squat has gotten weaker by the ratio, even though the bar got heavier — and a lifter who loses twenty pounds and keeps the same squat has gotten stronger, even though nothing about the bar changed. That is why the ratio is the measure, and why lifters who chase the ratio should think carefully about which side of it they change. Gaining weight to move up a level is a real strategy, but it is a strategy with a cost: every pound of bodyweight has to be carried through every rep, and the heavier you get, the lower the ratio you need to hit each level, because strength does not scale one-to-one with mass. The standards already reflect that — the absolute numbers in the table grow more slowly than the bodyweights — but the lifter still has to decide whether the weight gain is muscle or just mass, and whether the ratio actually improved.

There are also lifts the standards do not measure. A front squat, a belt squat, a box squat — each has its own standard, and none of them is the back squat standard in this guide. A lifter whose front squat is strong and whose back squat is ordinary is not a weak squatter. They are a lifter whose strengths run in a different direction, and the back squat table will not see it. Front squat technique and where it fits in a meet cycle is worth reading if the front squat is your better lift, because the variation that punishes a high squat is also the variation that builds the quads and the upright torso the back squat needs. And if your sport is not powerlifting at all — if you squat to be strong for something else — the standards are still useful as a benchmark, but they are not the verdict. A 1.5-times-bodyweight squat is strong in any context, whether or not anyone ever judged it.

How to compare your squat to your other lifts

A squat standard is more useful when it is read next to the other lifts, because the ratios between lifts tell you where the imbalance is. A lifter whose squat is intermediate but whose deadlift is advanced has a squat problem, not a strength problem — and the fix is usually squat volume, not more deadlift volume. A lifter whose bench is advanced and whose squat is novice has the opposite shape, and the fix is the same logic in reverse. The three lifts should roughly track each other by level, and the lift that lags is the one that gets the training attention. The DOTS calculator gives a single number for the whole total that adjusts for bodyweight and sex, and it is the better tool for comparing lifters against each other — but for comparing your own lifts against each other, the level-by-level standards in this guide are the sharper instrument.

There is one more comparison worth making, and it is the one lifters avoid: the squat against the clock. A lifter who has been intermediate for three years is not stuck because the standard is too hard. They are stuck because something in the training has not changed in three years — the program, the volume, the recovery, or the weak point that never got addressed. Standards are a snapshot, and a snapshot taken twice tells you whether you are moving. Take the measurement, write it down, and take it again in six months. If the level has not moved, the training is the thing to change, not the standard. The standard is doing its job. It is telling you the truth.

Using the standards to set your next goal

Pick the next level, not the level after that. A novice lifter whose next level is intermediate has a concrete target — for a 180-pound man, that is a 270-pound squat — and a concrete timeline: one to two years of consistent training with a real program. An advanced lifter whose next level is elite has a five-year target, and the honest thing to do with a five-year target is to break it into smaller ones: add ten pounds this cycle, hold the depth, fix the weak point, and let the level take care of itself. The standards work best as a direction, not as a deadline, because strength does not arrive on a schedule, and a deadline that passes without the level is a deadline that teaches the wrong lesson.

Set the goal in ratio terms, not in plate terms. A 180-pound lifter aiming for a 315 squat is aiming for 1.75 times bodyweight — solidly intermediate, closing on advanced — and that framing keeps the goal honest if bodyweight changes. A lifter who gains ten pounds and hits 315 has not hit the same goal as the lifter who stayed at 180 and hit 315. The ratio knows the difference. The plates do not.

And keep the goal in proportion. A lifter who squats 1.5 times bodyweight is stronger than half of everyone who trains, and stronger than almost everyone who does not. That is not a consolation prize. That is a real achievement, built over years of showing up, and the standards exist to make that achievement visible — to the lifter who built it, who is usually the last person to see it. Find your level. Respect it. Then go earn the next one.

Putting it together

Start this week. Test or estimate your one-rep max at parallel depth, filmed from the side, and divide it by your bodyweight. Find your level in the table — beginner, novice, intermediate, advanced, or elite — and write it down with the date. Then pick the next level and work out what it costs: the weight, the timeline, and the one change in your training that is most likely to get you there. If you are a novice, the change is probably consistency — three squat sessions a week, the same program, until it stops working. If you are intermediate, the change is probably programming — a real cycle, managed volume, and a weak point addressed on purpose. If you are advanced, the change is probably recovery — the sleep and the food that the heavy weights now demand.

Re-test in three to six months, the same way, under the same conditions, and compare the ratio, not the plates. If the level moved, the training worked. If it did not, change one thing — the program, the volume, or the recovery — and give it another cycle before judging. The standards will still be there, and they will still be honest. A squat that is 1.5 times your bodyweight today is a squat you built, and the next level is a squat you have not built yet. That is the whole game: find your level, respect it, and go earn the next one.
