Sumo Deadlift, Without the Apologies.

The sumo deadlift moves the same weight through a shorter pull with your torso upright — the setup step by step, the wedge, the five mistakes everyone makes, sumo vs conventional compared with no agenda, and how to program it from your first pull to meet peak.

Center Mass Strength

Why the Sumo Deadlift Exists

The deadlift is the only one of the big three with two legal stances, and sumo exists because the conventional pull is a leverage equation that not everyone wins. Conventional deadlift asks your body to hinge over a bar that sits six inches in front of your hips, with the range of motion set by your arm length and your back doing the bracing bill for the whole thing. If you're built long in the femurs and short in the torso, the conventional start position parks your hips in the sky and your torso nearly horizontal — you're pulling a lot of back and a little leg. Sumo rearranges that deal: feet wide, hands inside the knees, torso upright, hips close to the bar. The range of motion shortens. The back unloads. The hips and adductors take the job.

None of that is a cheat. Sumo is legal in every powerlifting federation on the planet — the bar leaves the floor, the knees and hips lock out, same rules as everyone else. The people who call it cheating are usually the people whose leverages make conventional easy, which is like a tall guy calling the front squat cheating because his femurs reach the ceiling. It's a stance, not a shortcut. The weight still has to move.

Who is sumo actually built for? Start with the long-femured, short-torsoed lifter — the build that spends its conventional pull folded in half. Then add the lifter whose lower back is the limiting system: a more upright torso means less shear on the lumbar spine, which matters enormously once you're north of 40 and the back's recovery budget is the whole program. Add lifters with genuinely strong hips and adductors who've never had a stance that let those muscles contribute. And add anyone who pulls conventional and stalls six inches off the floor, because the hardest part of the conventional pull — breaking it off the ground with a long lever arm — is the part sumo shrinks the most.

The honest version: some bodies are built to pull sumo, and they spend years grinding conventional because the internet told them sumo is for cheaters. Meanwhile their back hates every heavy session and their deadlift hasn't moved since 2022. If that's you, this guide is the permission slip. Pick the stance your skeleton was built for.

Conventional asks the back to pay for the pull. Sumo hands the bill to the hips. Pick the payer your body can afford.

What this guide covers: why sumo exists (and who it's built for), the setup step by step — stance width, foot angle, hip position, and grip: hook vs. mixed vs. straps — hip mobility demands and knee tracking, torso angle and the wedge, the five mistakes everyone makes, sumo vs. conventional compared honestly with no agenda, programming (sets, reps, RPE, where sumo lives in the macrocycle, how it pairs with squat work), sumo after 40 (hips, recovery, adductor care), and a four-week test to find out if sumo is actually for you. The Setup, Step by Step

Sumo setup is fussy, and that's a feature. The conventional pull is forgiving of sloppy positioning because the back can bail you out; in sumo, a lazy setup shows up the instant the bar leaves the floor — hips shoot up, knees cave, the whole thing falls apart. Do it the same way every time, in the same order, and the stance stops feeling like a circus act.

  • Set your stance width. Feet well outside shoulder width, and here's the rule that matters: when you sink down, your shins should be vertical when viewed from the front. Too wide and the knees can't stay out over the toes; too narrow and you're just doing a conventional pull with a wide foot placement. Start at roughly twice shoulder width and adjust by feel. Your body will tell you — the position where the hips can sit down between the feet without the back rounding is the right one.
  • Angle the feet out. Toes point out 30–45 degrees, roughly following the line of the knees. The feet follow the knees, not the other way around — set the knee direction first (out, over the toes) and the feet just accommodate it. Turn the feet too far out and you lose the foot's contact patch; too straight and the knees have nowhere to go.
  • Get the bar close. Bar over mid-foot, shins touching the bar or as close as your build allows. The whole point of sumo is shortening the lever arm between the hips and the bar — setup distance you leave on the table is range of motion you pay back at the hardest part of the pull. This is the single most skipped detail in bad sumo.
  • Sink the hips between the feet. Hips drop straight down between the heels until your hands can reach the bar — think sitting into the stance, not hinging back like a conventional pull. Chest proud, lats already starting to engage. If your hips can't get low enough without the back rounding, that's your mobility ceiling talking, and it's telling you to fix it before you load it (see the mobility section below).
  • Grip narrow, inside the knees. Hands go inside the knees, typically just outside the legs or roughly at shoulder width. The narrow grip is what keeps the arms from fighting the legs for space. Arms hang straight down from the shoulders — no reaching, no angle. Straight arms are long arms, and long arms are shorter range of motion.
  • Choose your grip: hook vs. mixed vs. straps. Hook grip (thumb pinned under the fingers) is the competition standard for sumo — symmetrical, no windmilling risk, and sumo's upright torso makes the hook slightly less punishing than on a bent-over conventional. It hurts for two to four weeks and then the thumb goes numb to it; that's the deal and it's worth it. Mixed grip works fine and most lifters already have it, but watch the supinated side's biceps — the arm hanging inside the knee has less room for error if the elbow wants to bend. Straps are for overload and volume work, never for testing; if your competition stance is strapless, most of your heavy work should be too.
  • Lock the lats and build tension. Before anything moves, pull your chest through and drag the bar into your shins — "bend the bar around your legs." The lats are what keep the bar glued to you through the pull. If there's slack between you and the bar, the start will be a yank instead of a pull, and yanks end with the hips shooting up.
  • Wedge, then push. Drive the knees out into your arms, push the floor away with your feet, and feel the tension build through the hips and adductors before the bar breaks the floor. The pull starts with pressure, not speed. The bar leaves the floor because the tension got too big to hold, not because you decided to rip it.

Take your time with steps seven and eight. New sumo pullers set up fine and then rush the start like it's a conventional pull — and a rushed sumo start is just a wide-stance good morning. The whole pull should feel like one continuous press of the floor, from tension to lockout.

Torso Angle and the Wedge

Here's the thing most people get wrong about sumo: it's not just a wide conventional deadlift. The torso angle is different — noticeably more upright — and that changes the entire loading strategy. In a conventional pull, the back is a major lever and the start is a hinge. In sumo, the upright torso means the back can't dominate even if it wants to; the hips, glutes, and adductors are the engine by geometry, not by choice.

The wedge is the mechanism that makes that geometry work. "Wedge" means driving your hips down and in toward the bar while pushing your knees out into your arms — you're physically wedging your body between the floor and the bar, loading the hips and adductors like springs before the pull starts. A good wedge looks almost boring: the lifter sinks in, gets quiet, and then the bar just starts moving. A bad wedge looks like a startle response — the hips pop up, the back takes over, and it becomes a conventional pull performed with the feet too wide.

You'll know you lost the wedge the moment your hips rise faster than the bar. Watch your own videos for this: if the first thing that moves is the hips climbing while the bar stays parked, you started the pull without tension. The fix isn't stronger hips — it's patience. Sink back in, rebuild the pressure through the floor and the knees-out push, and let the bar come up when the tension demands it. The pull should feel slow at the start. Slow is loaded. Fast is yanking.

Torso angle follows from the wedge. If you try to stay upright by arching the back, you'll hyperextend at lockout and the lower back will complain; if you let the torso collapse forward, you've given up the whole advantage of the stance. The chest stays proud because the lats are engaged and the wedge is holding the hips under the torso — upright because of position, not because of effort. It should feel almost like a leg press that starts from the floor: hips under, knees out, floor pushed away.

The Five Mistakes Everyone Makes

Same five, every gym. Sumo is unforgiving in exactly the places people are careless:

  • The hips shoot up first. The stripper pull, sumo edition. The hips climb while the bar sits still, and by mid-shin the lifter is in a wide conventional pull they didn't plan. The cause is always the same: no wedge, no lat tension, the start rushed. Fix: go back to the setup checklist, rebuild tension until the bar feels heavy in your hands before it leaves the floor, and video every heavy set until the hips and bar move as one unit.
  • The knees collapse inward. Under load the knees drift in toward each other, the adductors stop contributing, and the whole stance loses its structure. This is either weak glutes and adductors relative to the load, or a stance wider than the hips can own. Fix: lighten it and learn to drive the knees out hard into your arms on every rep — the arms are right there, use them as feedback. If the collapse only happens near max, the stance might be wider than your current hip strength can hold; narrow it an inch or two and build back up.
  • The bar starts too far away. Shins a hand's width from the bar, and the lifter pulls around their own knees. In sumo this is worse than in conventional because the upright torso gives the back less leverage to recover a bad line. Fix: every setup, drag your shins to the bar. It should feel uncomfortably close. That closeness is the advantage of the stance — don't set it up and then refuse to take it.
  • Yanking the bar off the floor. The setup looks fine, and then the lifter rips it like they're starting a lawnmower — slack everywhere, hips up, back rounded, the bar bouncing off the shins. The sumo start rewards patience and punishes aggression. Fix: treat every rep as a wedge rep. Pull the slack out, build the pressure, and let the floor push back. If the bar doesn't move for a full second of building tension, that's not a failed rep — that's correct.
  • Leaning back at lockout. The pull is clean, and then the lifter finishes by throwing the shoulders behind the hips and hyperextending the lower back, chasing a lockout that already happened. The lockout is hips through and knees straight — standing tall, glutes squeezed, not bent backward. Fix: think "tall" instead of "back." The judges need your knees and hips locked; they don't need to see your ceiling.

Hip Mobility Demands and Knee Tracking

Sumo is honest about one thing conventional lets you dodge: hip mobility. The conventional start position is a hinge — hips back, knees nearly straight — and most people can get into it regardless of hip structure. Sumo demands hip external rotation and adductor length under load, and if you don't have them, the stance will tell you immediately: the back rounds as you sink in, or the knees cave the moment you pull.

This is a gate, not a verdict. Test it simply: sink into your sumo start position with no weight and hold it for ten seconds. Can your back stay flat and your knees stay out over your toes? Then you have enough mobility to start loading it, and the loaded reps themselves will keep building it — the bottom of a sumo pull is a weighted hip mobility drill. If you can't hold the position — back rounds, knees collapse, or you just can't get the hips down — then address it before the weight gets serious: goblet squats with the elbows prying the knees out, the 90/90 hip switch, and adductor stretches done daily for a few weeks will open most people up. Mobility you can't own unloaded has no business under a bar.

Knee tracking is the live indicator. From the front, the shins should stay vertical and the knees should track out over the toes through the whole pull — never drifting inward, never wobbling. The cue that fixes most tracking problems is "knees into the arms": your arms are hanging inside your knees, so driving the knees out gives you instant physical feedback. If the knees are quiet and the shins are vertical, the hips are doing their job.

One warning nobody gives new sumo pullers: the adductors will be wrecked for the first two to three weeks. Muscles that have never been asked to do much are suddenly prime movers, and the soreness is the deep, walking-funny kind. This is normal, not an injury — but respect it. Start sumo at loads you could pull for sets of eight, not at your conventional max with a wider stance, and give the adductors the same progression patience you'd give any new movement. They'll catch up fast. The soreness is the receipt for muscles that were asleep.

Sumo vs Conventional, Honestly

Someone will tell you sumo is easier. Someone else will tell you it's cheating. Both of them are arguing about their own leverages. Here's the comparison with no agenda:

SumoConventional
Shorter range of motion — hips start close to the barLonger pull — the bar travels further from floor to lockout
Torso stays upright — less shear on the lumbar spineTorso leans forward — the back is a major lever and limiter
Hip and adductor dominant — glutes and inner thighs drive the pullPosterior-chain dominant — hamstrings and spinal erectors carry more
Hardest off the floor — breaking it loose is the sticking pointHardest below the knee — the bar stalls where the lever is longest
Demands hip mobility — the start position is a mobility gateDemands back strength — the start position is a bracing gate
Narrow grip inside the knees — less arm and shoulder involvementWider grip outside the knees — more lat and upper-back demand
Fully legal in every federation — same rules, same platformThe traditional standard — and equally legal, equally legitimate

The read is straightforward. Neither stance is "the real deadlift" — the real deadlift is the one that moves the most weight within the rules for your body. Long femurs and a short torso usually favor sumo; long torso and short arms usually favor conventional. A back that limits your training favors sumo; hips that won't open favor conventional. And plenty of strong lifters train both — one as the competition stance, the other as the variation that builds the muscles the competition stance neglects.

The test that settles it isn't a debate, it's a training block: run each stance heavy for six weeks and compare what moves at RPE 8, and which one leaves you recovered enough to train again in four days. The stance that wins both is your stance. Arguing about which is harder is what people do instead of pulling.

How to Program It

Sumo programs like any main lift — the stance changes the muscles, not the math. What changes is where it sits relative to your squat work and how the volume is distributed across the macrocycle.

Sets, reps, RPE. As a primary lift: 3–5 sets of 3–5 reps at RPE 7–9, the classic strength rep range. Sumo takes triples beautifully — the upright torso means less systemic fatigue per rep than conventional, so heavy triples are less costly than they look. As a secondary variation (if conventional is your competition stance): 3–4 sets of 6–8 at RPE 7–8, where it builds the hips and adductors without competing with the main pull. Cap top sets at RPE 9 like everything else. And the adductor warning applies double to programming: don't open a sumo block at your conventional volume — start at roughly two-thirds of your usual deadlift volume and build over three to four weeks while the inner thighs adapt.

Where it lives in the week. Sumo pairs naturally with squat work because it taxes the same systems the squat spares: pair it with a lighter or front squat day and the hips get hammered twice while the back stays fresh, or put it on its own lower-body day opposite your heavy squat. If you pull both stances, don't put heavy sumo and heavy conventional in the same week — pick one as the main pull and let the other be the lighter variation day. Two heavy deadlift stances in one week is how backs and hips file complaints.

Where it lives in the macrocycle. In the accumulation block, sumo is the volume engine: moderate weights, higher reps (5–8), building the hip and adductor base that heavy singles stand on. Deficit sumo pulls (standing on a plate or two) are the classic accumulation variation — they extend the hardest part of the pull and build brutal starting strength. In the strength block, the reps drop to triples and doubles at RPE 8–9, and the variations get specific: pause sumo pulls an inch off the floor for the break-off-the-floor sticking point, block pulls from just below the knee if the lockout is the issue. In the peak, everything narrows to the competition stance at competition weights — if sumo is your meet pull, the last weeks are all sumo, all specific, no variations. If the periodization guide's block structure is new to you, the short version: volume goes up early, intensity goes up late, and sumo does its best building work in the volume phase.

Frequency. Most lifters deadlift well once a week heavy, with a second lighter variation day if recovery allows. Sumo's lower back cost makes that second day more realistic than it is with conventional — a light sumo or deficit session mid-week is genuinely recoverable for a lot of lifters. But "the back feels fine" isn't the whole recovery picture: the hip girdle and adductors need their rest too, especially in the first two months of a sumo block. One heavy day and one light technique day is the ceiling for most people, and plenty of strong pullers do just fine on one.

Sumo After 40

If the conventional deadlift is the lift that ages lifters out, sumo is the stance that keeps them in. After 40, the recovery math changes: the lower back is the slowest-recovering system in the body, and the conventional pull taxes it more than any other lift. Sumo's upright torso doesn't eliminate back work — the spine still braces hard — but it cuts the shear demand dramatically, and for a masters lifter that difference is the difference between pulling heavy weekly and pulling heavy monthly.

Practically, three things. First, protect the adductors like they're a new lift, because they are. The inner-thigh soreness hits harder after 40 and lingers longer, and a strained adductor will sideline your squat and your deadlift at once. Start sumo embarrassingly light — weights you could triple without thinking — and add load only as fast as the soreness allows. The first month of a sumo block is mobility and tissue adaptation, not strength building, and rushing it is how masters lifters get hurt.

Second, make mobility maintenance non-negotiable. Hip external rotation doesn't maintain itself after 40. Five minutes a day — the 90/90 switch, a deep goblet squat hold with the elbows prying the knees out, some adductor stretching — keeps the start position available. Lose the position and the pull turns into the wide-stance good morning from the mistakes section, which is exactly the back-loading pattern sumo is supposed to avoid. The warm-up sets are part of this: two or three light sets of five, sinking deep into the wedge each rep, and the hips are ready.

Third, respect the new recovery pattern. Your back will feel better after sumo than after conventional — that's the point — but the hips, glutes, and adductors will be sorer than you're used to, and connective tissue recovers slower than muscle at this age. Don't max out weekly. Don't test both stances in the same month. And give a new sumo block a full eight weeks before judging it: the first month is adaptation, the second month is where the numbers start moving. How to Test If Sumo Is for You

Don't switch stances on theory. Run the test — four weeks, honest data, no arguing with the internet:

Weeks 1–4: sumo as the secondary pull. Keep your conventional as the main deadlift day. Add one sumo session per week: 3 sets of 6–8 at RPE 7, starting light enough that the positions are perfect. The goal of week one isn't weight — it's learning the setup checklist until it's automatic and letting the adductors introduce themselves.

Track four things. One: can you hit a clean start position by week three — flat back, knees out, shins vertical — without fighting it? Two: where does the bar feel slow — off the floor (sumo's hard part) versus below the knee (conventional's hard part)? Three: what's sore the next day — back (conventional pattern) or hips and adductors (sumo pattern)? Four: what moves more weight at RPE 8 by week four, on video, with honest judging of lockout?

Read the results without bias. If the positions came easy, the weight moved well, and your back feels better the day after — sumo is probably your stance, and it's worth a full training block as the primary pull. If the positions never clicked, the bar felt glued to the floor, and your hips are still angry in week four — your body is telling you conventional, and that's fine too. If it's close, run the test again with the stances flipped: six weeks of sumo as primary is the real verdict, because four weeks of secondary work only tells you whether the door is open, not what's behind it.

The point of the test is that the answer is in your body, not in a comment section. Plenty of lifters discover sumo adds thirty pounds to their pull in a single block. Plenty discover their conventional was right all along. Both are good outcomes — the only bad outcome is spending another year pulling a stance your skeleton hates because somebody on the internet has opinions.

Sumo Deadlift: Common Questions

Is sumo deadlift cheating?

No. It's legal in every powerlifting federation, under the same rules as conventional — bar to lockout, knees and hips extended, no hitching. The shorter range of motion is a leverage difference, the same way long arms are a leverage advantage in conventional. Calling it cheating is arguing with geometry. Pull the stance your body is built for.

How wide should my sumo stance be?

Wide enough that your shins are vertical when viewed from the front at the start position — that's the rule that sets the width for your body. For most lifters that's roughly twice shoulder width, but hip structure varies: too wide and the knees can't stay out over the toes, too narrow and it's just a wide conventional. Start at double shoulder width, sink in, and adjust until the hips can sit between the feet with a flat back and vertical shins.

Will sumo deadlift hurt my hips?

It will make them sore — the adductors and hip external rotators become prime movers, and the first two to three weeks bring deep soreness. That's adaptation, not injury. Actual hip pain (sharp, pinching, in the joint) is different: it usually means the stance is wider than your hip structure allows, or you're forcing depth your mobility doesn't own. Narrow the stance, address the mobility gate, and build up. Joint pain is information — don't train through it.

Should tall lifters pull sumo?

Height alone doesn't decide it — proportions do. A tall lifter with long femurs and a short torso is the classic sumo build, because conventional folds them in half at the start. A tall lifter with a long torso and long arms might pull conventional beautifully. The test in this guide settles it: run both stances, compare what moves at RPE 8, and check which one leaves you recovered. Let the barbell vote.

Hook grip or mixed grip for sumo?

Hook grip is the competition standard — symmetrical, no biceps risk on the supinated side, and sumo's upright torso makes it slightly less brutal than on conventional. It hurts for a few weeks and then the thumb adapts. Mixed grip works and most lifters already have it, but keep the arms dead straight: the arm inside the knee has no room for a bent elbow. Straps are for volume and overload work, not for testing.

How often should I deadlift sumo?

Once a week heavy, with an optional second lighter variation day — deficit pulls or pause pulls at RPE 7 — if recovery allows. Sumo costs the back less than conventional, which makes the second day more realistic, but the hip girdle still needs rest, especially in the first two months. One heavy day plus one light technique day is the ceiling for most lifters.
