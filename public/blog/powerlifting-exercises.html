Powerlifting only tests three lifts. Every other exercise in your program exists to make those three go up — or it's noise. Here's the full roster: the squat, bench, and deadlift, the accessories that actually move the total, how to pick them by weakness, and what to skip. With the over-40 swap list.

Center Mass Strength

Powerlifting is the simplest strength sport there is: squat more, bench more, deadlift more. Add the three numbers together and that's your total. Every exercise you'll ever do as a powerlifter falls into one of two buckets — the three competition lifts themselves, and everything else, whose entire job is making those three go up. Lifters get into trouble when they forget that. They fall in love with an accessory, run it for months, and can't figure out why the total didn't move. The accessory was fine. It just wasn't serving the total. This guide sorts the whole roster: the big three, the accessories that earn their place, how to pick them by weakness instead of by trend, and the noise to skip — plus the over-40 swap list for joints that have opinions.

The big three: the only lifts that count

Everything starts here. The squat, the bench press, and the deadlift are the sport. They should be the heaviest, most practiced, most respected movements in your training, and nothing else in the program ever outranks them. If an accessory is getting more of your attention than the main lift it feeds, the program is upside down.

The squat. Bar on your back, hips below parallel, stand back up. Low-bar is the powerlifting standard — the bar sits lower on the rear delts, which lets you lean forward slightly and move more weight — but high-bar and front squats all feed the same pattern. If your squat technique needs work, start with the barbell squat form guide before you touch a single accessory. Most stuck squats are technique problems wearing a strength costume.

The bench press. Back on the bench, bar to the chest, press to lockout. It's the lift lifters most often treat as a chest exercise — it isn't. It's a full-body skill: leg drive, a tight arch, lats pulling the bar to the right touch point. The guys with the biggest benches almost always have the biggest upper backs, which tells you most of what you need to know about bench accessories.

The deadlift. Bar off the floor to lockout, the heaviest thing you'll ever lift. Conventional or sumo — both are legal, both count, and the right choice is the one that fits your build. If you're on the fence, the sumo deadlift guide walks through the decision honestly, and the deadlift form guide covers the setup that both stances share.

The training rule for all three: they get the most practice, the heaviest loading, and the freshest version of you. Main lifts first, accessories after. Always.

Why accessories exist at all

Here's the case for accessories, stated plainly: the big three make you strong at the big three, but they don't always fix the specific spot where you fail. If your deadlift dies two inches off the floor, more heavy deadlifts from the floor will help — but deadlifts starting from a deficit attack the exact weak point. If your bench stalls halfway up, close-grip work builds the triceps that finish the rep. Accessories are targeted medicine. The good ones fix a diagnosed weakness. The bad ones are just exercises you enjoy.

And that's the selection rule: pick accessories by weakness, not by trend. Where does the lift actually break down? Off the floor, off the chest, out of the hole, at lockout, halfway up? Find the sticking point and pick the exercise that loads exactly that range. An accessory that doesn't correspond to a weakness you actually have is a hobby, not a training tool. There's nothing wrong with hobbies — just don't confuse them with the program.

The accessories that actually move the total

This is the short list — the exercises with the longest track record of adding pounds to competition lifts. Every one of them has a clear job.

Squat accessories. The front squat builds the quads and the upper back that keep you upright out of the hole; it's also the best squat-pattern teacher in the gym, because sloppy front squats don't survive. Pause squats — a two-count pause at the bottom — kill the stretch reflex and build real strength where most squats die, just above parallel. The belt squat loads the legs hard with zero spinal loading, which makes it the best heavy squat-pattern volume you can add without beating up your back. And box squats teach you to sit back and engage the posterior chain instead of diving forward — useful if your squat turns into a good morning.

Bench accessories. The close-grip bench press is the king: it builds the triceps that finish every heavy rep, and it carries over almost one-to-one. Pause bench — pausing on the chest like the competition commands — builds control off the chest and kills the bounce that inflates gym numbers. The floor press overloads the top half and hammers triceps, and it's joint-friendly besides. For the upper back that a real bench is built on: barbell rows, chest-supported rows, and pull-ups, done heavy and often. The bench press is a back exercise that happens to move the bar with your arms — train the back like it.

Deadlift accessories. Deficit deadlifts — standing on a plate or two — build strength off the floor where conventional pullers usually fail. Rack pulls and block pulls overload the top half and the lockout for lifters who get the bar moving and then die. The Romanian deadlift builds the hamstrings and glutes with a fraction of the systemic fatigue of pulling from the floor, which means you can actually recover from it week after week. And good mornings, done conservatively, build the lower-back and hamstring strength that keeps a heavy deadlift from folding you in half.

The always-on accessories. Two categories belong in every powerlifting program regardless of weaknesses: upper-back work (rows of every kind, face pulls, pull-aparts) because all three lifts are stabilized by the upper back, and core work (heavy carries, ab rollouts, hanging leg raises) because a strong trunk is what lets force transfer from the legs to the bar. Neither one is glamorous. Both pay rent on the total.

The ones that are mostly noise

Let's be straight: some popular exercises have a terrible return on investment for powerlifters. The leg extension and leg curl isolate the quads and hamstrings but teach nothing about moving weight through a full pattern — they're bodybuilding finishers wearing a strength costume. Endless chest flyes don't build a bench; close-grip pressing does. Smith machine versions of the big three let the machine stabilize the bar for you, which is exactly the part your body needs to learn. And the bosu-ball-everything crowd is training balance, not strength.

The deeper noise problem isn't a specific exercise — it's exercise roulette. Switching your accessories every two weeks because you saw a new one on the internet. An accessory needs four to eight weeks of consistent work before you can judge whether it fixed the weakness. Lifters who rotate constantly never find out what works, because nothing got a fair trial. Pick two or three per lift, run them for a full block, then judge by one metric: did the main lift move?

How to program accessories (without wrecking the week)

Accessories are seasoning, not the meal. The programming rules that keep them in their place:

Two to three accessories per lift per week is plenty. Most lifters need one pressing accessory, one pulling accessory, and one squat-pattern accessory in rotation, not a twelve-exercise arm day. If your accessory list is longer than your main lifts, you're majoring in the minors.

Accessories never get maxed. This is the rule that separates lifters who last from lifters who flame out. The main lift gets the heavy loading and the PR attempts. Accessories live in the submaximal range — 70 to 85%, controlled reps, leaving a couple in the tank. The moment you start grinding accessory singles, the accessory has become a second main lift, and your recovery is now paying for two programs. That's how people get hurt doing exercises that were supposed to be safe.

Put them after the main lift, in weakness order. The most important accessory goes first, while you're freshest. Upper-back and core work can go last — they don't need you fresh, and they'll still get done.

Match the accessory to the program block. In a volume block, accessories are higher-rep builders (sets of 8–12). As a meet approaches, they narrow to heavier, more specific work (sets of 3–6) or get trimmed entirely. The powerlifting program guide walks through how the whole week fits together — accessories are one piece of that puzzle, not the puzzle itself.

The over-40 swap list

After 40, the joint math is real, and some exercises that were fine at 28 start sending invoices. The answer isn't to stop training hard — it's to swap the exercises that beat you up for ones that deliver the same stimulus with less joint cost.

If the back squat beats up your lower back or shoulders: the belt squat loads the legs brutally with no spinal compression — it's the single best squat swap in existence. The front squat keeps you upright and takes the lower back out of the equation, though it asks more of the wrists and upper back.

If heavy benching irritates the shoulders: the floor press limits the range of motion to the joint-friendly top half and still builds a huge lockout. The close-grip bench is usually kinder to shoulders than wide-grip maxing. And dumbbell pressing lets the shoulders track naturally instead of locking them to a bar path.

If pulling heavy from the floor wrecks you: block pulls and rack pulls keep the heavy loading with a shorter, friendlier range of motion. The trap bar deadlift is the kindest heavy hinge in the gym — and while it's not a competition lift, it builds the exact muscles your deadlift needs. The Romanian deadlift at moderate loads is hamstring and glute work you can do every week without paying for it on Friday.

The principle: stimulus over ego. The muscle doesn't know what exercise you did. It knows tension, range of motion, and recovery. After 40, pick the version of every pattern that you can run for twenty weeks straight without a flare-up. Consistency at 85% beats heroic at 100% followed by six weeks of physical therapy. Every time.

Five mistakes that waste your exercise selection

1. Copying an elite lifter's accessory list. The 700-pound bencher's program was built for his weaknesses, his joints, and his recovery capacity — which are not yours. His list is a case study, not a prescription. Diagnose your own sticking points.

2. Maxing accessories. Said above, saying again because it's the most common one: the accessory that gets ground out for singles is now a main lift, and your program has a second job it can't afford. Submaximal, controlled, done.

3. Skipping upper-back work because it's boring. Rows don't get filmed. Nobody asks your row max. But the upper back is the platform every heavy lift launches from, and lifters who neglect it stall everywhere at once. Do the boring work.

4. Fixing technique with accessories. If your squat caves because your knees don't track, a new accessory won't fix it — practicing the squat with better technique will. Accessories build strength. Practice builds skill. Don't mix up the tools. When the main lift is technically broken, the main lift is the project.

5. Judging an accessory after two weeks. Strength adaptations take four to eight weeks to show up in the main lift. The lifter who swaps accessories every fortnight is running a new experiment every fortnight and learning nothing. Commit to the block. Judge at the end.

The bottom line

The roster is short on purpose: three lifts that count, a handful of accessories that fix real weaknesses, upper-back and core work that never stops, and nothing else until those are handled. Pick your accessories by where your lifts actually fail, keep them submaximal and consistent for a full block, and swap the joint-hostile versions for friendly ones when the years demand it. The lifter with five well-chosen exercises run for months will bury the lifter with twenty exercises rotated weekly. Serve the total. Everything else is noise.
