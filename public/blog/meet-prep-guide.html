The Complete Guide to Powerlifting Meet Prep.

The meet date is the one fixed point in your training. Everything below is how to build toward it: peak without maxing, taper on purpose, choose attempts like math, and execute when the lights are on.

Center Mass Strength

You train heavy four days a week. You know your squat, your bench, your deadlift to the pound. And then meet day arrives, and the bar you smoke in your garage pins you in front of fifty people, because the meet is not a heavy gym day with judges. It is a different sport wearing your sport's clothes.

The difference is everything around the bar. Fixed schedule. Flight order. Commands you have to wait for. Warmup room math with twenty other lifters. Nine attempts across eight hours, each one a pass/fail test you cannot retake. The lifters who total well are not always the strongest in the building; they are the ones whose prep accounted for all of it.

Training builds strength. Meet prep builds the conditions to display it.

Meet prep is a system with five parts: the calendar (picked backward from the date), the peak (sharpening without maxing), the taper (draining fatigue on purpose), attempt selection (math, not ego), and meet-day execution (the schedule, the warmups, the third-attempt call). This guide covers all five, in order, with the numbers that actually matter.

What this guide covers: why competition is its own skill, picking the meet and building the calendar backward, how to peak without maxing, what the taper actually cuts (and keeps), weigh-in and weight management without drama, the attempt-selection math, and the meet-day execution plan. If you lift on CMS's SBD Full Powerlifting track, the engine already runs this calendar for you.

Why Competition Is Its Own Skill

A training rep has one judge: you. A competition rep has three, plus a head judge issuing commands, plus a clock, plus a flight of lifters whose warmups set your pace. The squat must reach depth. The bench press must pause on the chest, wait for the start command, wait again for the press command, then wait for the rack. The deadlift has a down command. None of that exists in your garage.

Then there is the attempt structure. Three shots per lift, nine total, and the weights only move in one direction per lift. Miss your opener and the meet gets hard in a hurry: you are burning a second attempt to do what the first one should have done, your confidence drops, and your remaining attempts all carry the pressure of the first miss. Bomb out of one lift (three misses) and your total is zero, no matter what the other two lifts did.

This is why meet prep cannot be "train hard and show up." The research on powerlifting tapers makes the same point from the data side: when elite raw powerlifters were studied in detail, their final weeks looked deliberately engineered, not just "hard training continued," with total volume peaking about five weeks out and intensity peaking under two weeks out (Pritchard et al., 2016). The last month is not a smaller version of training. It is a different phase with a different job.

Pick the Meet, Work Backward

Everything in meet prep starts from the date, and the date has to give the whole chain room to exist. Working backward: one week of taper, four weeks of peak, then the strength and accumulation work that makes the peak display something real. That is five fixed weeks minimum before you count a single week of actual building.

The practical rule: do not pick a meet closer than 60 days out if you want a real prep. CMS's SBD track enforces exactly this floor, because below it there is no room for the sequence to do its job. Twelve to twenty weeks out is the comfortable range for most lifters. Masters lifters, whose recovery runs on a slower clock, should lean toward the longer end, since every phase of the chain takes them a little more time to absorb.

Choosing the meet itself is the one decision that needs no overthinking. Pick a local novice-friendly meet, full power, at a federation whose rules you can read in an afternoon. Your first meet is about learning the event, not setting records. The federation's rulebook (commands, depth, equipment allowed) is your actual competition; read it before you register.

Once the date is set, the calendar assembles itself. The full mechanics of building that backward calendar are in The Complete Guide to Powerlifting Periodization: peak and taper fixed at the end, accumulation protected at the start, strength blocks filling the middle. Meet prep is that guide's application layer.

The Peak: Peak Without Maxing

The peak is the most misunderstood phase in powerlifting. Lifters hear "peak" and think "heaviest weeks," which is exactly wrong. The peak is where training becomes competition-specific: heavy singles, doubles, and triples on the competition lifts, with competition commands and competition equipment, while volume drops. You are not building strength in the peak. You are teaching your nervous system to display the strength you already built, on command, under meet conditions.

The data backs this shape. In the Pritchard study of elite powerlifters, total training volume peaked about 5.2 weeks out while average intensity peaked only about 1.9 weeks out: the heaviest-feeling work lands late, but the most work happened a month earlier (Pritchard et al., 2016). Intensity climbs while volume falls. That is the peak in one sentence.

Five rules for running it:

  • Train the competition lifts like the competition. Pauses on bench, depth on squat, commands wherever you can get them. Meet-day technique should be boring by the time you arrive.
  • Peak on intensity, not volume. Keep top-end loads high (roughly 90 to 95 percent for the main singles) and cut the backoff work. The volume that built you is done paying dividends.
  • Do not test your max in the peak. A grinder at 100 percent in week three of the peak spends the recovery you need in week one of the meet. The meet is the test; everything before it is rehearsal.
  • Protect the deadlift. Elite lifters report that deadlifting takes longer to recover from than the other lifts, and most pull their last heavy deadlift a full week to ten days before the meet, ahead of the other lifts' last heavies.
  • Strip the accessories late. About two weeks out, drop the assistance work that built your base and keep only what directly serves the platform. The assistance served its purpose months ago.

If the strength is not there when the peak starts, no amount of heavy work in this phase will conjure it. That is the honest limit of the peak: it reveals, it does not build. Which is why the calendar protects the accumulation and strength phases before it, and why a bad week in the peak gets the autoregulated treatment rather than a panicked extra session.

The Taper: Cut Volume, Keep Intensity

The taper is where you stop piling on and let the fatigue you deliberately built all cycle drain away. It is one week in the CMS structure, and it has one of the most consistent evidence bases in all of training science: the classic meta-analysis on tapering found performance gains concentrating in a short, deliberate window of reduced volume, not a long coast (Bosquet et al., 2007).

What the taper literature converges on: reduce total volume by roughly 30 to 60 percent, maintain or slightly increase intensity, keep training frequency roughly normal, and cease hard training two to four days before competition (review in Sports, 2020). The powerlifting-specific data matches: elite lifters cut volume by about 59 percent during the taper while holding intensity, and took their last weight session about 3.7 days out (Pritchard et al., 2016). Every source says the same thing in different words: less work, same weight, fresher lifter.

The taper week, in practice:

  • Cut volume, not load. Drop sets and reps across the board. The loads stay heavy; the totals shrink.
  • Keep the competition lifts in. Squat, bench, deadlift stay, performed cleanly, at loads that keep the nervous system tuned to heavy weight.
  • Drop everything else. Accessories, variation work, extra conditioning: gone for the week. They were for building. You are done building.
  • Do not take a full week off. Elite lifters consistently report that poor tapers come from too long off training, not too little. A week of couch is detraining wearing a taper costume.
  • Protect sleep and food. The taper is when recovery actually lands. Sleep like it is training, because it is.

The taper is the phase where doing less takes the most discipline. If the taper feels wrong, like you should be doing more, it is working. The work is done. Let it show.

Weigh-In and Weight Management

Every federation weighs you before the meet, and the weigh-in window changes the whole equation. A 24-hour weigh-in gives you a day to rehydrate and refuel after making weight; a 2-hour weigh-in gives you almost none, which means you step on the platform at essentially the weight you step on the scale.

The honest hierarchy, in order of sanity:

  • First meet: compete where you are. Do not cut a pound. Your first meet is a skill event (commands, attempts, pacing), and every unit of attention spent on the scale is stolen from the platform.
  • Experienced lifters: small cuts only, 24-hour weigh-ins only. A few pounds of water, recovered fully in a day, is the sane ceiling for a lifter whose training did not depend on the cut.
  • Never cut into a 2-hour weigh-in. Without a recovery window, the cut is just strength you left in the bathroom.

Aggressive water cuts are a separate skill with real dehydration risks, and they have ended more meets than they have saved. This is general training information, not medical advice: if you are considering a serious cut and you have a managed condition, talk to a clinician first. For everyone else, the best weight class is the one you train at.

What you eat once the weigh-in is behind you matters more than the weigh-in itself. The fueling protocol for the long day, what to pack, and what to eat between attempts is covered in Meet-Day Nutrition, the fueling-side companion to this guide.

Attempt Selection: Math, Not Ego

Attempt selection is where meets are won and lost, and it is the one part of meet prep that is almost pure arithmetic. Each lift gets three attempts. The opener's only job is to put you on the board. The second attempt is your real number. The third is earned or skipped based on how the second moved.

The standard that has survived decades of platform experience:

  • Opener: a weight you could triple on your worst day. Roughly 90 to 92 percent of your planned second attempt, a single at about RPE 8. It should move like a warmup with judges watching.
  • Second: your goal, the number you came for. Roughly your gym best or a small PR, a single at about RPE 9 to 9.5. This is the attempt the whole meet is built around.
  • Third: the reach, and only if the second earned it. A fast second with white lights means you take the jump. A grinding second means you repeat or take a token increase. A missed second means the third attempt is a repeat, period.

Two principles govern all of it. First, a missed opener is the costliest mistake in powerlifting: you burn your second attempt redoing the first, your confidence drops, and every attempt after carries the first miss. Second, conservative openers build bigger totals than aggressive ones, because they buy a confident second and a real decision on the third. The 3-for-9 perfect meet is a nice outcome, not a plan. The plan is: get on the board, hit your number, then see what the day allows.

The RPE logic behind these calls, what "one more rep in the tank" actually looks like at 95 percent, is the same skill the training uses every session. If RPE as a language is new to you, RPE for Powerlifting builds it from the ground up.

One rule you can act on tonight: write your openers now, in pencil, as the weight you could triple on your worst training day. If the number on the card makes you nervous, it is your second attempt, not your opener.

Meet-Day Execution

The meet is a long day with a fixed rhythm, and the rhythm is set by flights, not by you. You lift when your flight lifts. Your job is to arrive at each lift warm, fed, calm, and with a plan that survives contact with the day.

  • Warm up to the clock, not to your feelings. Know the flight order and lifter count; start your warmups so your last warmup lands about 10 to 15 minutes before your opener. Twenty lifters ahead of you is plenty of time. Three is not.
  • One lift at a time. When you are squatting, you are not thinking about the deadlift. The meet is nine separate events that happen to share a day.
  • Learn the commands before you need them. Squat: wait for "squat," then "rack." Bench: "start," then "press," then "rack," with a real pause on the chest. Deadlift: "down." Practice them in the peak so they are boring on the day.
  • Eat and drink on schedule. Eight to ten hours, steady grazing, nothing new. The fueling system is in Meet-Day Nutrition; run it from the packing list.
  • Protect the third attempt decision. Decide the third from the second's speed and the judges' lights, not from the leaderboard. The lifter who repeats a grinder and hits it totals more than the lifter who jumps and misses.

Powerlifting Meet Prep: Common Questions

How far out should I pick a powerlifting meet?

Give the full prep chain room to exist: a peak phase, a taper, and the strength work underneath them. CMS builds meet-anchored macrocycles from a minimum of 60 days out, with the final five weeks locked in as four weeks of peak plus one week of taper. Twelve to twenty weeks out is the comfortable range for most lifters, and masters lifters should lean toward the longer end.

How do I taper for a powerlifting meet?

Cut volume sharply while holding intensity. Research on powerlifting tapers shows volume reduced by about 30 to 60 percent with intensity maintained, accessory work dropped roughly two weeks out, and the final weight session performed three to four days before the meet. Do not take a full week off and do not test your max mid-taper; the meet is the test.

What should my opener be in a powerlifting meet?

A weight you could triple on your worst day: roughly 90 to 92 percent of your planned second attempt, or a single at about RPE 8. The opener's only job is to put you on the board. Conservative openers build bigger totals because they buy a confident second and a real decision on the third.

Should I cut weight for a powerlifting meet?

For your first meet, no: compete at the weight you walk around at. For experienced lifters, only a small cut, and only with a 24-hour weigh-in to recover. Aggressive water cuts are a separate skill with real dehydration risks; if you have a managed medical condition and are considering one, talk to a clinician first.

Can I do a full power meet as my first competition?

Yes, and it is the best first meet: three attempts each at squat, bench, and deadlift gives you the full event in one day. Pick a local novice-friendly meet, open light, learn the commands and the rhythm, and let the first meet be about execution rather than numbers.
