Carbs for Powerlifting: Fueling Heavy Training Days.

Heavy sessions run on glycogen, not motivation. How to eat for the heaviest days on your program: before, during, and after, with amounts you can actually follow.

Center Mass Strength·14 min read

Ask a room of powerlifters what they eat before a heavy squat day and you’ll get two religions. One group treats carbs like contraband: fasted mornings, black coffee, pride in the empty stomach. The other treats the pre-workout like a cargo ship, loading a pile of pasta at noon for a 6 p.m. session, then a sports drink, then a gummy bears break between squats and deadlifts.

Both groups are answering the wrong question. The question isn’t whether carbs are good or bad. It’s whether the fuel in the tank matches the session on the calendar. Heavy training days are the highest-fuel sessions on a powerlifting program, and the lifter who fuels them correctly gets something no supplement sells: session quality. The top set moves at the planned RPE instead of a surprise grinder. The back-off sets get done instead of abandoned. And Thursday’s session doesn’t pay for Wednesday’s.

The uncomfortable truth underneath: a lot of “bad training days” are nutrition days wearing a costume. You showed up to a heavy session on Tuesday’s leftovers.

What Carbs Actually Do in a Lifting Session

Your muscles store carbohydrate as glycogen, roughly 300–400 grams in muscle plus another 80–100 grams in the liver, depending on size and diet [1]. When you squat, bench, and deadlift heavy, you spend it. One study showed six sets of 12RM leg extensions reduced glycogen in the working muscle by 39% [1]. That’s one exercise. Add a second and a third, at competition-relevant loads, and the drain adds up fast.

When glycogen runs low, the ability to maintain intensity and work output drops, and markers of tissue breakdown rise [1]. That’s the mechanism behind most mid-session fades. The set that “felt heavy for no reason” at RPE 9 when it was supposed to be RPE 8 wasn’t a mystery. It was a muscle telling you the fuel ran short.

This is why carbohydrate availability is the lever that moves session quality. Research on athletic fueling defines the question exactly this way: is the carbohydrate substrate limiting your session, or is it adequate? Availability goes up with intake before the session, during it, and in recovery between sessions [2]. Day-to-day training quality depends on restoring those glycogen stores between sessions, which requires enough dietary carbohydrate and enough time [3].

Here’s the No-BS line that keeps this post honest: the specific carbohydrate requirements for resistance exercise have not been conclusively established. Most of the published numbers were built for endurance athletes and extended downward. The numbers below are the best evidence-based guidelines we have, scaled to powerlifting by volume and session length. They’re starting points to individualize, not commandments.

Myth vs. Reality

MythReality
Carbs before lifting make you sluggish.A carbohydrate-containing meal 1–4 hours before training is the standard guideline for sessions over 60 minutes [2]. “Sluggish” usually means the wrong food (high fat/fiber) or the wrong timing, not the carbs.
Training fasted builds more discipline and burns more fat.Fasting before heavy work trades session quality for a feeling of toughness. Low carbohydrate availability is a documented performance limiter [2]. Discipline is showing up fed and executing the plan.
You only need protein after training; carbs don’t matter for lifters.Glycogen is the main fuel your heavy sets spent, and restoring it is what lets you train again at quality [3]. Protein builds; carbs refuel. The session needs both.
Intra-workout carbs are only for marathon runners.Carbohydrate ingestion during resistance training has been shown to maintain blood sugar and keep glycogen stores higher during multi-set lifting [1]. Sessions running past 60–70 minutes earn fueling.
Post-workout carbs have to hit within 30 minutes or the session is wasted.The anabolic window is wider than the shaker industry suggests: pre- and post-exercise protein intake show similar effects on adaptations, and total daily intake matters most [4]. If you train again within 8 hours, refuel aggressively; otherwise, your next real meal covers it [2].
Keto works fine for powerlifting. Fat is fuel.Fat is fuel for low-intensity work. Heavy compound sets are glycolytic: they spend glycogen, and you cannot lipolyze your way through a 5×5 at 85%. High-intensity anaerobic work is where carbohydrate restriction costs you the most.

The Framework: Before, During, After

Daily: scale carbs with training volume. The guidelines map daily carbohydrate to training load: roughly 3–5 g/kg for light or skill days, 5–7 g/kg for moderate training around an hour, 6–10 g/kg for high training loads of 1–3 hours, and 8–12 g/kg for very heavy training commitments [2]. Heavy squat and deadlift days sit in the 5–7 g/kg band; lighter bench or accessory days sit lower; rest days sit lowest. A 90-kg lifter on a heavy day is looking at roughly 450–630 grams of carbohydrate across the day, which three big meals with a real carb anchor plus a peri-workout snack will get most of the way toward. The g/kg numbers size carbs against bodyweight, but the full-day picture needs a calorie target. If you don’t know yours, the WizMeals TDEE calculator gives you a starting estimate: set protein and fat first, then let carbs fill the remaining calories.

Before: 1–4 g/kg, 1–4 hours out. For sessions longer than 60 minutes, eat 1–4 g/kg of carbohydrate in the 1–4 hours before training [2]. Closer to the session, go smaller and simpler: a banana or a sports drink 45–60 minutes out. The foods should be ones you know and tolerate. The guidelines explicitly say to avoid high fat, high fiber, and unfamiliar choices right before training because gut distress is the failure mode [2]. This is not the meal to experiment with.

During: sessions past 60–70 minutes earn 30–60 g/hour. The ISSN review recommends ~30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour in a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution for extended high-intensity bouts, especially those spanning beyond 70 minutes [1]. Practical options: a sports drink diluted to taste, a banana between movements, a handful of gummy bears or a gel. The point is maintaining blood glucose and sparing muscle glycogen so the back-off sets don’t decay [1]. For shorter, sharp sessions around an hour, even small carbohydrate amounts, including carbohydrate mouth rinsing, can aid performance through central nervous system effects [5].

After: refuel fast only when you train again soon. If less than 8 hours separates two fuel-demanding sessions, the guideline is aggressive: 1–1.2 g/kg/hour for the first 4 hours [2]. For a 90-kg lifter, that’s roughly 100 g of carbohydrate per hour, a deliberate refeeding protocol rather than a snack. The ISSN version of the same advice: 1.2 g/kg/hour with a preference for high-glycemic sources, optionally combined with 0.2–0.4 g/kg/hour of protein [1]. If your next session is tomorrow, none of this urgency applies. Eat a normal protein-and-carb meal within a few hours and let the daily total do the work. The evidence for a narrow 30-minute “window” is weak, and total intake carries the outcome [4].

One Heavy Day, Fueled Correctly

Here’s a heavy lower-body day for a 90-kg lifter training at 5:30 p.m.: squat top sets, deadlift volume, accessories, about two hours in the gym.

12:30 p.m.: the anchor meal. Roughly 150 g of carbohydrate: a large bowl of rice (about 120 g carbs from ~150 g dry rice), chicken breast, some vegetables, light sauce. This is the 1–4 g/kg pre-session meal landing ~3.5 hours before training, about 1.7 g/kg for this lifter, comfortably inside the guideline [2]. Lunch matters more than any pre-workout product: this meal stocks the glycogen the session will spend.

4:30 p.m.: the top-off. One banana plus a 500 ml sports drink, about 55 g of carbohydrate. Small, fast, familiar. Nothing new on heavy days.

5:30–7:30 p.m.: the session. A diluted sports drink sipped between movements: roughly 30–40 g of carbohydrate over the session. The session runs two hours, so intra-workout fueling earns its place [1]. The squat top sets move at the planned RPE, and the deadlift back-offs happen at the planned volume instead of getting cut.

8:00 p.m.: the recovery meal. Rice or pasta, lean protein, vegetables: another 100–120 g of carbohydrate with 40–50 g of protein. Nothing exotic. The next session is in 48 hours, so there’s no refeeding emergency. This meal starts the glycogen restoration the next heavy day depends on [3].

Day’s total: roughly 380–400 g of carbohydrate, about 4.5 g/kg. On a higher-volume week the same lifter pushes toward 500+ g by enlarging the breakfast and lunch anchors. The carbohydrate total follows the training volume, always. That is the same idea as your nutrition is a skill: practiced like any other, scaled like any other. The tedious step is turning this into a week of real meals instead of one perfect example day. Seven Principles for Fueling Heavy Days

1. Fuel the day, not the hour. Session-day carbohydrate starts at breakfast, not at the pre-workout shaker. The guideline numbers for daily intake (5–7 g/kg on heavy training days) exist because glycogen is stocked across the day, not in a single meal [2]. A huge pre-workout meal can’t rescue a day of eating nothing.

2. The heavy session gets the biggest carb meal. Match your largest carbohydrate meal to the hardest session, 3–4 hours before it. Lighter days and rest days need less. The stimulus goes up, so the support goes up with it.

3. Pre-train inside the guideline: 1–4 g/kg, 1–4 hours. Familiar foods, low fat, low fiber, nothing new [2]. The closer to the session, the smaller and simpler. If you’re over 40 and your digestion has opinions about big pre-session meals, splitting the pre-session carbs into lunch plus a small top-off usually solves the problem. The after-40 nutrition picture covers the age-specific adjustments.

4. Sessions over 70 minutes earn intra-workout carbs. Aim for 30–60 g/hour from a sports drink or simple carbs during long sessions [1]. The payoff is in the back-off work: maintaining blood glucose means the sets after the top set get done at the intended effort instead of decaying into grinders [1]. A grinder you didn’t need is fatigue you pay for on Thursday.

5. After training, urgency depends on the calendar. Training again within 8 hours? Refuel at 1–1.2 g/kg/hour for 4 hours [2]. Training tomorrow? Eat a normal meal within a few hours and make sure the daily total lands [4].

6. In a deficit, protect heavy-day carbs. Cutting weight is when most lifters slash carbs everywhere and watch their top sets crumble. If you must reduce energy, take it from rest days and lighter sessions first. The heavy session’s carbohydrate is the load-bearing wall: it’s what keeps submaximal work staying honest instead of turning every heavy day into a survival day. Calories are flexible; session fuel isn’t.

7. Carbs are an input, not an excuse. Fueling correctly raises the ceiling on what a good session can be. It doesn’t rewrite the program. If a set is prescribed at RPE 8 and it moves like RPE 8, you hold, even well-fueled. The readiness check-in still runs, and the check-in reads the day whether you ate well or not. Fuel gives the day its best version. It doesn’t override the system.

One rule you can act on tonight: Anchor your heaviest session tomorrow with a real carbohydrate meal 3–4 hours before you train. For a 90-kg lifter that’s roughly 90–180 g of carbohydrate: a big bowl of rice or pasta with protein, or two large bagels with eggs and fruit. Not a snack, not a pre-workout scoop. One deliberate meal, timed once. Watch what your top set costs this week versus last.

Frequently Asked Questions About Carbs for Powerlifting

How many carbs should a powerlifter eat per day?

Scale it to the training: roughly 5–7 g/kg on heavy training days, less on light days, less again on rest days [2]. A 90-kg lifter on a heavy day is looking at about 450–630 g. Don’t start there if you’re currently eating half that. Add a carb anchor to each meal for a week, then adjust based on how your sessions feel.

Should I eat before morning training?

If the session is longer than an hour, yes. The guideline explicitly addresses this with the 1–4 g/kg, 1–4 hours rule [2]. At 6 a.m. you can’t eat 3 hours out, so you adapt: a small, fast carbohydrate (a banana, a sports drink, white toast with honey) 30–45 minutes before, then a bigger breakfast after. Early morning lifters are the group intra-workout carbs help the most.

Do I need carbs during my workout?

Only if the session runs long enough to spend them. A focused 45-minute bench session needs water, not fuel. For a two-hour lower-body session with volume work, 30–60 g/hour of carbohydrate during the session helps maintain blood glucose and glycogen status [1].

What about low-carb or keto for powerlifting?

Be honest about the trade. Heavy compound sets are glycolytic: they spend muscle glycogen fast (one study measured 39% depletion in a working muscle after six sets of leg extensions [1]). Restricting carbohydrate limits the fuel those sets run on [2]. Some lifters maintain strength on keto, but the evidence base for high-intensity anaerobic performance favors adequate carbohydrate availability. If you go low-carb anyway, expect the heavy volume days to cost more and schedule accordingly.

Are sports drinks better than real food around training?

They’re different tools. Before and after, real food is better: it carries protein, micronutrients, and fiber alongside the carbs. During the session, a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte drink is genuinely useful because it’s fast, it hydrates, and it doesn’t sit in your stomach [1]. Use food for meals and fluid carbs for the session.

Do carbs matter for masters lifters specifically?

The fueling mechanics don’t change with age. What changes is everything around them: appetite often drops, protein gets the attention, and carbs quietly disappear from the plate. If you’re over 40 and your heavy days have been fading, check carbohydrate first. It’s the cheapest variable to fix.

Medical note: This is general training and nutrition information, not medical advice. Carbohydrate needs change with medical conditions. Diabetes, insulin resistance, and other metabolic conditions all alter the picture. If you have a managed condition or unexplained symptoms, work with your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing how you eat.
