Every list ranks a different program first, and every list is right — for somebody else. 5/3/1, Sheiko, Candito, GZCL, and the Texas Method are all proven programs with real lifters and real totals behind them. The question is not which one is best. The question is which one fits your level, your schedule, and your recovery.

Center Mass Strength

Ask ten strong lifters for the best powerlifting program and you will get ten answers, each delivered with total confidence. The 5/3/1 lifter will tell you it is the only program that has ever worked for him. The Sheiko lifter will show you a spreadsheet with four hundred sets in it. The Candito lifter will show you the free PDF that took his total up two hundred pounds. They are all telling the truth, and none of them is answering your question, because your question is not really about the program. It is about you: how long you have been training, how many days you can train, what you are training for, and how much recovery your life actually allows. A program is a plan for adding weight over time, and the best plan is the one you can follow for months without stalling, getting hurt, or quitting. That is the whole test, and it has very little to do with which famous name is on the cover.

This guide compares the programs lifters actually ask about — 5/3/1, Sheiko, Candito, GZCL, the Texas Method, and the Juggernaut Method — on the things that decide whether a program works for you: who it is built for, how many days it takes, how the weight goes up, and what it costs in time and recovery. It also covers the honest way to pick, the way to run whatever you choose, and the mistakes that turn a good program into a bad result. If you want the full picture of how programs are built before you pick one, the complete guide to building a powerlifting program from the ground up covers frequency, volume, and progression in detail. This post is the shorter question: which named program, for which lifter?

The short version

There is no best program, only a best fit. A beginner does not need Sheiko. An advanced lifter does not need linear progression. A lifter with three days a week does not need a five-day program, and a lifter with a bad back does not need the program with the most deadlift volume. The program that wins is the one matched to the lifter running it, and the match matters more than the name on it. Every proven program does the same four things. It tells you which lifts to train, how much volume to do, how the weight increases, and what to do when a week goes wrong. If a program cannot answer all four, it is not a program. It is a workout list, and a workout list is what you are trying to escape. Run your pick for at least two full cycles before judging it. One cycle is not a test. The first cycle of any program is mostly learning the structure, and the numbers that matter show up in the second and third. A lifter who switches programs every six weeks has not tried five programs. He has tried five first weeks.

What a program actually is

Before comparing names, it helps to say what the names are selling. A powerlifting program is a plan for getting stronger at the squat, the bench press, and the deadlift over weeks and months, and every real program is built from the same four parts. Progression is the rule for when the weight goes up: add five pounds every session, add weight every four-week cycle, or add reps until a target is hit and then add weight. Volume is how much work you do: sets and reps per lift per week. Frequency is how often each lift is trained. Specificity is how close the training is to the competition lifts — a program that trains the competition squat, bench, and deadlift most of the time is more specific than one built on machines and variations. The famous programs differ mostly in how they set those four dials, and the differences are smaller than the arguments about them suggest.

That is the first honest point: the top programs overlap far more than they differ. All of them train the three lifts. All of them use submaximal weights for most of the work, with the heavy testing saved for the end of a cycle. All of them build in some form of deload or easy week, because no program survives without one. The lifter who runs 5/3/1 for a year and the lifter who runs Sheiko for a year will both be stronger, and the difference between their totals will have more to do with consistency, sleep, and food than with the name of the program. The complete guide to powerlifting periodization and how training blocks fit together shows why: the structure underneath all of these programs is the same — build volume, push intensity, peak, test, repeat — and the named programs are just different ways of drawing that shape. Pick the drawing you will actually follow.

The second honest point is that a program is only as good as the lifter's ability to recover from it. Volume is a cost, and the cost is paid in sleep, food, and time. A program that asks for five days a week and fifteen hard sets of squats is not a better program than one that asks for three days and eight sets. It is a more expensive program, and whether it is worth the price depends on what you can afford. This is the point lifters over 40 learn the hard way, and it is the reason the right program for a 25-year-old with no kids is often the wrong program for a 45-year-old with a job, a mortgage, and a back that remembers every bad decision. Recovery is not an excuse. It is a variable, and the program has to be solved with the variable included.

How to judge any program before you run it

Five tests, applied to any program, will tell you most of what you need to know before the first session.

Does it have a progression rule you can state in one sentence? Add five pounds a week. Add weight every four-week cycle. Hit the target reps, then add weight. If the rule takes a paragraph to explain, the program is either very sophisticated or very confused, and for most lifters it is the second one. Simple progression is not a sign of a simple program. It is a sign of a program that was designed to be followed by a tired person at 6 AM, which is when programs actually get followed.

Does the volume fit your week? Count the hard sets per lift per week, and count the days. A program that needs five days when you have three is not a program you will run. It is a program you will modify, and a modified program is a different program — usually a worse one, because the modifications are made on the fly by the person the program was supposed to protect from their own instincts. Pick a program that fits the days you actually have, not the days you wish you had.

Does it train the competition lifts? A powerlifting program should spend most of its heavy work on the squat, the bench press, and the deadlift, done the way you will do them on a platform. Variations are useful — close-grip bench, pause squats, block pulls — but they are accessories, and a program that spends more time on variations than on the lifts themselves is a program that has lost the plot. Specificity is what turns gym strength into platform strength.

Does it build in a deload or an easy week? Every proven program has one, and the reason is not caution. It is arithmetic: fatigue accumulates, and a program that never backs off will stall or break the lifter before the cycle ends. If a program has no planned easy week, the easy week will arrive anyway, as a missed session, a sore back, or a week off that was not in the plan. Planned rest beats forced rest every time.

Has it worked for lifters like you? A program proven on elite 25-year-olds is not proven on a 48-year-old beginner, and a program proven on beginners is not proven on an advanced lifter chasing a 500-pound deadlift. Look for lifters at your level, your age, and your schedule who ran it for a full cycle and got stronger. That is the evidence that matters. A famous name is not evidence. A testimonial from a lifter with your constraints is.

5/3/1 — Jim Wendler

5/3/1 is the most run powerlifting program in the world, and the reason is not that it is the most effective. It is that it is the most followable. Jim Wendler published it in 2009, and the structure has barely changed since: four lifts — squat, bench press, deadlift, overhead press — each trained once a week in a four-week cycle. Every percentage is calculated from a training max set at 90 percent of your true one-rep max, not from the max itself, which is the program's core idea: train submaximally, most of the time, and let the weight climb slowly. Week one is 65, 75, and 85 percent for sets of 5, 5, and 5-plus reps. Week two is 70, 80, and 90 percent for sets of 3, 3, and 3-plus. Week three is 75, 85, and 95 percent for 5, 3, and 1-plus. Week four is a deload at 40, 50, and 60 percent. The plus sign means as many good reps as you have got on the last set. After each cycle, add five pounds to the upper-body training maxes and ten pounds to the lower-body ones, and run it again.

The strength of 5/3/1 is its honesty about speed. Five pounds a month on the bench and ten on the squat does not sound like much, and that is the point: it is progress you can actually sustain for years, and a year of it is sixty pounds on the press and a hundred and twenty on the squat and deadlift, if the cycles hold. The weakness is the same slowness. A lifter who wants fast progress, or who is peaking for a meet in eight weeks, will find the monthly wave too patient. The other weakness is the low frequency: each lift once a week means less practice than the high-frequency programs, and lifters whose technique needs work sometimes stall on skill, not strength. The fix is in the variants — Boring But Big adds volume, 5/3/1 for Beginners adds frequency — but the base program is what it is: simple, slow, and still working after fifteen years. The guide to submaximal training and why you should not max out all the time explains the principle 5/3/1 is built on, and it is worth reading before you judge the weights as too light. The weights are light on purpose. That is the program.

Sheiko — Boris Sheiko

Sheiko is the opposite of 5/3/1 in almost every way, and it is the program lifters mention when they want to sound serious. Boris Sheiko coached the Russian national team, and his programs are built on a simple belief: strength is a skill, and skill is built by doing the competition lifts often, with moderate weights, for a lot of sets. A Sheiko week can have you squatting, benching, and deadlifting three or four times each, mostly between 70 and 85 percent, with total weekly volume far above what most programs ask for. The sessions are long — two hours is normal — and the weights feel almost too light for the first few weeks, until the accumulated volume catches up with you and the fourth set of five at 80 percent feels like a max.

Sheiko works, and the reason it works is the frequency: practicing the lifts that often makes the technique automatic, and automatic technique is what holds up under a heavy single. It is also the program most likely to break a lifter who runs it without the time and recovery to match. The volume is a real cost, and it is paid in hours and in soreness, and a lifter with a demanding job, a family, and forty-plus years on the clock will often find that the cost exceeds the return. Sheiko is the right program for an intermediate or advanced lifter with the schedule to train four long days a week, the patience to trust submaximal weights, and the recovery to absorb the volume. It is the wrong program for a beginner, who does not need that much practice yet, and for a busy lifter, who will run it for three weeks, miss a session, and quit. The programs on this list are all proven. Sheiko is the one most likely to prove it by breaking you first.

Candito 6-Week — Jonnie Candito

The Candito 6-Week program is the best free program on this list, and it is the one most lifters should try first when they outgrow beginner training. Jonnie Candito built it as a complete six-week cycle: four to five days a week, squat and bench trained with real frequency, deadlift programmed carefully because it is the most fatiguing lift, and a max test in week six that tells you whether the cycle worked. The structure is periodized in the classic sense — volume first, intensity later, a test at the end — and the whole thing fits on a single spreadsheet that Candito gives away. That matters, because a program you can see in full before you start is a program you can trust, and a program you trust is a program you will finish.

The strength of Candito is the built-in test. Most programs ask you to trust the process and check in months later. Candito gives you a date, six weeks out, when you find out what the cycle did. That deadline is motivating, and it is honest: a program that cannot show results in six weeks is a program that is not working. The weakness is the schedule: four to five days a week is more than many lifters have, and the deadlift volume, while carefully managed, is still real work for a lifter whose back is the limiter. Candito is the right program for an intermediate lifter with four or five days a week who wants a complete, free, testable cycle. It is the wrong program for a lifter who can only train three days, or for a lifter who needs a longer runway than six weeks to make progress. If you are choosing between Candito and 5/3/1, the honest difference is time: Candito asks more of your week and tells you sooner whether it worked. 5/3/1 asks less and takes longer to prove itself. Pick by your calendar, not by your preference.

GZCL — Cody Lefever

GZCL is the program for the lifter who wants structure and freedom at the same time, which is a combination most programs do not offer. Cody Lefever built it on a tier system: T1 is the main lift, trained heavy for low reps; T2 is a close variation, trained for moderate reps; T3 is accessories, trained for high reps. The progression is simple and self-correcting — the last set of the main lift is pushed for reps, and the number of reps you get decides whether the weight goes up, stays, or comes down. That rule is the program's real contribution: it removes the guesswork from progression without removing the lifter's judgment, because the reps you actually hit are the data, and the data decides.

The strength of GZCL is its adaptability. The tier system is a framework, not a fixed routine, so a lifter can swap the T2 and T3 work to attack a weak point — a close-grip bench for a weak lockout, a pause squat for a weak bottom position — without breaking the program. The weakness is the same freedom: a lifter who does not know what their weak point is will fill the T2 and T3 slots with whatever looks fun, and the program quietly becomes a random workout with a heavy main lift attached. GZCL rewards a lifter who can diagnose, and it punishes a lifter who cannot. It is the right program for an intermediate lifter who knows their weak points and wants a system that lets them attack those points directly. It is the wrong program for a beginner, who does not have weak points yet — they have everything to improve — and for a lifter who wants to be told exactly what to do. If you want the exact sets and reps written out, run Candito. If you want a framework and the freedom to fill it, run GZCL.

Texas Method — Mark Rippetoe

The Texas Method is the program lifters run when linear progression stops working, and it is the shortest step from beginner to intermediate training. Mark Rippetoe's design is three days a week: Monday is volume day, five sets of five at a weight that is hard but repeatable; Wednesday is a light recovery day; Friday is intensity day, a heavy set of five that sets the new PR. The Monday volume drives the Friday PR, the Wednesday light day lets you recover enough to hit it, and the following Monday's volume is set from the Friday number. It is a weekly cycle instead of a daily one, and that one change — adding weight once a week instead of once a session — is the entire difference between beginner and intermediate programming.

The strength of the Texas Method is its clarity. The weekly rhythm is easy to understand, easy to follow, and easy to measure: did Friday go up? Then the program is working. The weakness is the volume day, which is brutally hard once the weights get heavy — five sets of five squats at a real weight is a session that takes everything, and it takes more out of an older lifter than a younger one. Many lifters run the Texas Method for a few months, hit the wall on Monday, and move on, which is fine: the program is a bridge, not a destination, and a bridge that gets you across is a bridge that worked. It is the right program for a lifter in their first year or two of serious training who has just stalled on adding weight every session and has three days a week to train. It is the wrong program for a lifter who is already well past intermediate, because a weekly PR stops being sustainable once the weights get truly heavy, and the volume day becomes a recovery problem instead of a stimulus. The complete guide to a powerlifting program for beginners covers the linear progression the Texas Method replaces, and the handoff between the two is the moment a lifter stops being a beginner.

Juggernaut Method — Chad Wesley Smith

The Juggernaut Method is the program for the lifter who likes long, patient waves and does not mind waiting for the heavy weights. Chad Wesley Smith built it in four waves — 10s, 8s, 5s, then 3s — each wave lasting four weeks, with the reps dropping and the intensity climbing as the waves go on. Each wave ends in a rep-out test set that recalibrates the training max for the next wave, so the program is always working from your actual performance rather than a number you set months ago. The full run is sixteen weeks, which makes it the longest commitment on this list, and the slowest to show a heavy single.

The strength of Juggernaut is the patience it enforces. Sixteen weeks of waves builds a base of volume and work capacity that shorter programs skip, and the lifters who finish it usually find their heavy weights feel easier, not just heavier. The weakness is the length: sixteen weeks is a long time to wait for a test, and a lifter who needs a meet peak in eight weeks, or who gets bored easily, will not finish it. Juggernaut is the right program for an intermediate or advanced lifter with a long off-season and no meet on the calendar, who wants to build a base and is willing to trust the process for four months. It is the wrong program for a lifter with a meet in ten weeks, or for a lifter who has never run a full program cycle before — sixteen weeks is too long a first commitment. Run Candito or 5/3/1 first. Run Juggernaut when you know you can finish what you start.

What about linear progression, and what about adaptive programming?

Two more options sit outside the named-program list, and both matter. The first is linear progression — add five pounds every session, three days a week, the Starting Strength model — and it is the best program in the world for a true beginner, and a bad program for everyone else. A lifter in their first three to nine months can add weight every session because their body is still learning the movements and the weights are still light enough to recover from quickly. That window closes, usually within a year, and the lifter who keeps trying to add weight every session after it closes is the lifter who stalls, gets hurt, and concludes that programming does not work. Programming works. Linear progression just has an expiry date, and recognizing it is the first intermediate skill.

The second is adaptive programming, which is what CMS itself does: instead of a fixed spreadsheet, the program adjusts to the lifter's actual numbers and recovery, week to week, using the same principles this guide describes — progression, volume, frequency, specificity — but set from the lifter's logged data rather than a template. The honest case for it is that no template knows your sleep, your stress, or your joints on a given week, and a program that adjusts to those things will beat a program that ignores them, especially for a lifter over 40 whose recovery varies more than a 25-year-old's. The honest case against it is the same as the case against any system you do not understand: if you cannot say why your program changed the weight this week, you are trusting a black box, and trust should be earned by results, not assumed. The test is the same as for any program on this list. Run it for two or three cycles. Did the numbers go up? Then it works, whatever is inside it.

How to pick: by level, by days, by goal

Picking is easier when you sort by the three constraints that actually decide it. Start with level. A beginner, under a year of consistent training, should run linear progression, and nothing on this list is better for them — not because the named programs are bad, but because a beginner does not need periodization yet. They need practice and weight on the bar, every session. An intermediate, one to three years in, should pick from 5/3/1, Candito, GZCL, or the Texas Method, by schedule and temperament: three days and simple means Texas Method or 5/3/1; four to five days and a test date means Candito; a framework you can tune means GZCL. An advanced lifter, three-plus years in with a real total, should look at Sheiko or Juggernaut, or at adaptive programming, because at that level the template is the limitation and the program has to fit the lifter, not the other way around. The complete guide to an intermediate powerlifting program and the 4-day template covers the handoff from beginner to intermediate in detail, and the handoff is the moment this choice matters most.

Then sort by days. Three days a week means the Texas Method, 5/3/1 on a three-day schedule, or linear progression if you are still a beginner. Four days means 5/3/1 as written, GZCL, or Juggernaut. Five days means Candito, or a Sheiko schedule if the sessions can be long. The number of days is not a preference. It is a hard constraint, and a program that needs five days from a lifter who has three is a program that will be run badly. Pick by the days you actually have, and be honest about them: the days you train when life is normal, not the days you train in a perfect week.

Then sort by goal. A meet in eight to twelve weeks means a program with a peak built in — Candito, with its week-six test, or a Sheiko competition block, or a meet-prep cycle. General strength with no meet on the calendar means 5/3/1, GZCL, or Juggernaut, because the timeline does not matter and the sustainable program wins. Hypertrophy alongside strength means GZCL, because the tier system has room for the accessory volume, or a powerbuilding split — and the honest comparison of the two goals is in the complete guide to bodybuilding vs powerlifting and how the training actually differs, because the goal decides the program more than the program decides the goal. A lifter who wants a bigger total and a lifter who wants bigger arms are running different programs, even if both programs use a barbell.

How to run it properly

Picking is the easy part. Running it is the work, and the rules for running any program on this list are the same. First, run it as written for at least two full cycles. The first cycle is orientation: you are learning the structure, the weights are estimates, and the numbers are noisy. The second cycle is the test: the weights are calibrated, the structure is familiar, and the results are real. A lifter who changes a program after one cycle has not tested it. They have tried it on, and a program is not a shirt.

Second, do not add volume to a program that is working. The most common way lifters break a good program is by adding sets, adding days, or adding accessories, because the program feels too easy in week two. It feels easy because it is supposed to: the hard weeks are at the end of the cycle, and the easy weeks are what make the hard weeks possible. Adding volume to a program that is working is like adding weight to a bar that is already moving — it feels productive and it ruins the plan. If the program is working, leave it alone. If it is not working after two full cycles, change one thing, not five, and give it another cycle before judging.

Third, track everything, and use a real progression rule instead of a feeling. The RPE scale is the tool for this, and the complete guide to RPE for powerlifting and rating your sets honestly shows how to use it without lying to yourself: a set that felt like an 8 when it was a 10 is a set that will cost you next week. Write down the weight, the reps, and how the set actually felt, and let the log decide the next jump. The program is a plan. The log is the evidence. The lifter who has both is the lifter whose total goes up.

Fourth, take the deload. Every program on this list has one, and every lifter on this list skips it at least once. The deload is not a wasted week. It is the week that lets the previous three weeks turn into strength instead of fatigue, and skipping it is the fastest way to turn a working program into a stalled one. The guide to deloads vs one-day adjustments and when to back off covers how to tell the difference between a planned deload and a bad day, and the short version is this: a bad day is one session, a deload is a week, and you should be doing the second one on schedule whether you feel like you need it or not.

The mistakes that turn a good program bad

Program-hopping. Six weeks of 5/3/1, then six weeks of Sheiko, then six weeks of Candito is not a year of training. It is three first cycles, and first cycles do not build totals. The lifters with the biggest totals are usually the lifters who ran the same program, or the same family of programs, for years. Boredom is not a reason to switch. A stall after two full cycles is.

Maxing every week. A max is a test, and a test repeated weekly stops being a test. It becomes the training, and training made of maxes is training made of fatigue. The programs on this list test at the end of a cycle, not at the end of a week, and the reason is that the test costs recovery that the training needs. Test when the program says to test. Train in between.

Running the advanced program too early. Sheiko for a beginner is not ambitious. It is a volume load a beginner cannot recover from, on technique a beginner has not learned yet. The program ladder exists for a reason, and skipping rungs does not get you to the top faster. It gets you injured, and an injured lifter is not running any program at all.

Ignoring the weak lift. Every lifter has a lift that lags, and every program on this list assumes you will train it anyway. The lifter who skips deadlift day because deadlifts are hard is not running 5/3/1. They are running a bench-and-squat program with a deadlift-shaped hole in it, and the hole is where the total goes missing. Train the lift you are worst at. That is the lift the program is for.

Copying someone else's numbers. The percentages in these programs are calculated from your max, not from the max of the lifter who wrote the article you read. A training max set from someone else's numbers is a program set for someone else's body, and it will either be too light to matter or too heavy to survive. Test your own max, set your own training max, and run your own program.

Putting it together

Start with the three questions: what level are you, how many days do you have, and what are you training for? A beginner with three days runs linear progression. An intermediate with four days and no meet runs 5/3/1 or GZCL. An intermediate with five days and a test date runs Candito. An advanced lifter with a long off-season runs Sheiko or Juggernaut. A lifter whose recovery varies week to week, and who is tired of templates that ignore it, looks at adaptive programming. Those are the honest answers, and they are boring in the best way: boring answers are the ones that work, because they are the ones you can follow for a year.

Then run it as written, for two full cycles, and write down every number. The program is not a magic spell. It is a plan for adding weight over time, and the plan works when the lifter works the plan — the same sessions, the same progression rule, the same deload, week after week, until the cycle ends and the test says what happened. If the numbers went up, run it again. If they did not, change one thing and run it again. That is the whole secret of the best powerlifting program, and it is the same secret behind every program on this list: the best program is the one you actually finish, and the lifter who finishes programs is the lifter whose total keeps moving.
