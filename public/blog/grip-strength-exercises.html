← The Strength Blog GRIP STRENGTH · COMPLETE GUIDE Your Grip Is the Weak Link. Here Is How to Fix It.

Every lifter meets the same ceiling eventually: the legs and back can pull the weight, but the hands cannot hold it. The bar rolls, the dumbbells slip, the carry ends early — not because you are weak, but because your grip gave out first. This guide covers the three kinds of grip strength, the exercises that build each one, how to program grip work without wrecking your main lifts, and a weekly plan that turns a weak link into a non-issue.

Center Mass Strength

Grip strength is the most honest kind of strength there is. You either hold the weight or you do not, and no amount of technique talk changes the answer. Most lifters discover their grip the same way: a deadlift that flies off the floor and then slips out of their hands at lockout, a set of rows cut short by forearms that burn out before the back does, or a farmer carry that ends because the fingers opened, not because the legs quit. The frustrating part is that grip is almost never trained on purpose. It is expected to just show up as a side effect of lifting, and for a while it does — until the weights get heavy enough that the side effect stops being enough. Then grip becomes the ceiling on everything else, and the fix is not mysterious. It is a handful of exercises, done consistently, in the right dose.

The short version

Grip strength is three strengths, not one. Crush grip is closing your hand hard, the kind you use on a gripper or a thick bar. Support grip is holding on for time, the kind you use on a deadlift, a pull-up bar, or a farmer carry. Pinch grip is holding something between your fingers and thumb, the kind you use on plates or a block. Train all three and your grip gets strong everywhere; train only one and you will still drop things in the other two. Most lifters are weakest in support grip, because that is the one their main lifts actually demand.

The best grip exercises are simple, heavy, and boring. Dead hangs, farmer carries, thick-bar holds, plate pinches, and gripper work cover almost everything, and they cover it better than a drawer full of gadgets. You do not need a new tool every month. You need a few movements you can load, track, and progress, the same way you progress a squat. A grip exercise you cannot measure is a grip exercise you cannot improve.

Program grip at the end of sessions, two to three times a week, in small doses. Grip work is fatiguing in a sneaky way: fried forearms make the next day's pulling feel terrible, and a grip session done before deadlifts will cost you deadlift weight. Put it last, keep it to ten or fifteen minutes, and progress it slowly. Grip adapts well to steady work and badly to heroic single sessions, because the hands and tendons recover more slowly than the muscles around them.

The three kinds of grip

Crush grip is the one people picture when they hear "grip strength": squeezing something shut. Grippers, thick bars, and heavy dumbbell holds all build it, and it carries over to anything you have to clamp down on. It is also the easiest kind to overdo, because crushing feels productive and the forearms pump up fast. A little goes a long way, and the dose that builds crush grip is smaller than the dose that leaves your elbows aching for a week.

Support grip is holding a heavy weight without letting it slip. This is the grip that decides deadlifts, and it is the one most lifters actually need. A deadlift does not fail because you cannot squeeze hard enough in a single burst; it fails because your fingers slowly open over five or ten seconds under load. Support grip is trained by time under load: hangs, holds, and carries where the goal is not to squeeze harder but to not let go. If your deadlift grip is the problem, support work is the answer, not more grippers.

Pinch grip is the odd one out, and it is worth training precisely because nothing else trains it. Holding plates smooth-side-out, pinching a block, or carrying a hub by its edge loads the thumb in a way a barbell never does. Thumb strength is the hidden half of grip: a strong thumb keeps the fingers closed around a bar, and a weak thumb is why a bar rolls even when the fingers feel strong. Pinch work also has the most everyday carryover of any grip training, because real life is full of things you pick up by pinching, not by wrapping a hand around a bar.

The practical point is not to specialize early. A beginner grip program hits all three in small doses, and only later — once you know which one actually limits you — do you lean into one. A powerlifter whose deadlift slips needs support work first. A climber or a manual worker who handles odd objects needs pinch work too. A lifter who just wants a stronger handshake can live on crush work and carries. The diagnostic question is simple: what do you drop, and how does it slip? The answer tells you which kind of grip to train.

Why grip is the ceiling on your other lifts

A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and in most pulling, the hands are that link. Your back and legs can outgrow your grip by a wide margin, because they get trained with big compound movements every week while grip gets whatever is left over. That gap is invisible at light weights and obvious at heavy ones. The deadlift form guide covers the setup that keeps a bar from rolling, but no setup fixes a hand that cannot hold the load. When the grip goes, the set ends, and the back and legs never get the stimulus they were there for.

Grip also leaks into lifts you would not expect. Rows, pull-ups, and pulldowns all end early when the forearms quit first, which means the back gets less work than the program intended. Even pressing is affected: a bar that sits loosely in the hand because the wrist and grip are weak is a bar that wobbles, and a wobbling bar is a press that never feels stable. Strong hands make every barbell lift feel more connected, because the bar stops being something you are fighting to hold and starts being something you are simply moving.

There is a second reason grip matters, and it has nothing to do with the gym. Grip strength is one of the most useful kinds of strength you can own outside it: carrying groceries, opening jars, holding a tool, catching yourself on a rail. It is also one of the first strengths to fade with age if it is never trained, and one of the easiest to keep, because the dose is so small. A lifter who trains grip twice a week is not just protecting deadlifts. They are protecting the everyday strength that makes an independent life easier at every age, and that is a return most assistance work cannot offer.

That said, keep the claim honest. Grip training will not add fifty pounds to your deadlift by itself, and a gripper routine will not fix a deadlift that fails off the floor for a technique reason. What grip training does is remove the ceiling. Once the hands stop being the limiter, the lifts that were being held back can finally be trained properly, and that is where the real progress comes from — not from the grip work itself, but from the training it unblocks.

The exercises that actually work

Dead hangs. Hang from a pull-up bar with a full grip, arms straight, shoulders packed, for time. This is the simplest support-grip exercise in existence and one of the best, because it loads the grip with your whole bodyweight in exactly the position a pull demands. Start with three sets of thirty seconds and build toward sixty. When sixty seconds is comfortable, add weight with a dip belt. The common mistake is hanging with a loose, open grip or swinging like a pendulum; keep the grip full, the body still, and the time honest.

Farmer carries. Pick up two heavy dumbbells or handles, stand tall, and walk. Carries build support grip under movement, which is the closest thing to real-world grip there is, and they build the traps, core, and posture along the way. Use a weight you can carry for thirty to forty meters before the grip starts to open, and rest fully between sets — three to four sets is plenty. Short, heavy carries build grip; long, light walks build conditioning and call it grip. Do not confuse the two.

Thick-bar holds and lifts. A thicker bar forces the hand open wider, which makes the same weight far harder to hold. If your gym has a fat bar or fat grips, use them for holds, rows, or deadlift variations at a reduced weight. The load has to drop — a thick bar at your normal deadlift weight is a recipe for a dropped bar — but the grip stimulus per pound is much higher. One thick-bar movement per week is enough; more than that, and the elbows start complaining before the grip improves.

Plate pinches. Hold two smooth plates together, smooth sides out, between your fingers and thumb, for time or for carries. Start with two ten-pound plates for thirty seconds and build up in weight and time. Pinch work is humbling: weights that look trivial on a bar feel enormous between a thumb and fingers, and that is the point. Keep the plates from sliding by squeezing through the thumb, not by pressing them against your leg, and stop the set when the plates start to slip rather than grinding through a dropped plate.

Gripper work. A quality adjustable gripper, closed for reps or held shut for time, is the most direct crush-grip tool there is. The key is to buy one you can actually close for five to eight clean reps, not one you can barely budge — grippers are ego traps, and a gripper you cannot close teaches you nothing. Three sets of eight to twelve reps, or holds of ten seconds at the top, twice a week, is a solid dose. Warm the hands up first, and keep the reps clean: full close, controlled open, no cheating the last inch with the other hand.

Barbell holds and timed deadlift holds. Load a barbell to just above your deadlift working weight, lift it to lockout, and hold it for time. This is the most specific support-grip exercise for a powerlifter, because it is the deadlift grip under deadlift load, minus the pull. Ten to twenty seconds per hold, three sets, at the end of a deadlift session, is plenty — and it doubles as a confidence builder for lockout. Use straps on your actual deadlift work sets if grip is the limiter that day, and save the grip training for after; a training session is not the place to let a weak link cap your main lift.

Wrist curls and reverse wrist curls. These are not glamorous, but the wrist extensors and flexors stabilize the hand under load, and weak wrists let a bar roll in a way that looks like a grip problem. Two sets of fifteen to twenty reps, light, at the end of a session, keeps the wrists healthy and the forearms balanced. Keep the weight modest and the range full; wrist work done heavy and sloppy is how elbows get sore, and sore elbows end grip programs faster than any plateau.

What about the gadgets — the spring rings, the finger bands, the endless forearm rollers? Some are fine, and finger extension bands in particular are a useful counterbalance to all the closing work. But gadgets do not replace the big movements above. A grip program built on a gadget collection is a grip program built on novelty, and novelty stops working the week the novelty wears off. Pick the six movements above, track them, and let the gadgets be seasoning, not the meal.

How to program grip work

Put grip work last. This is the single most important programming rule, because grip fatigue bleeds into everything after it. A grip session before deadlifts costs you deadlift weight. A grip session before rows costs you row volume. A grip session at the end costs you nothing, because there is nothing after it that needs fresh hands. The only exception is a dedicated grip day, and most lifters do not need one until grip is a genuine competitive priority.

Two to three sessions a week, ten to fifteen minutes each, is the right dose for most lifters. A sample week looks like this: Monday after squats or bench, three sets of dead hangs and three sets of plate pinches. Wednesday after deadlifts, three sets of farmer carries and two sets of wrist work. Friday after upper-body work, three sets of gripper work and one thick-bar movement. That is six movements across the week, none of them long, all of them tracked. If a session runs long, cut a set, not a main lift — grip work is assistance, and assistance never eats the main course.

Progress grip like any other lift: add a little weight, a little time, or a little distance each week, and write it down. A dead hang that goes from thirty seconds to forty-five is progress, even though no weight changed. A farmer carry that goes from forty meters to fifty at the same weight is progress. A gripper you close for eight reps this month and twelve next month is progress. The exercises guide makes the same point about assistance work generally: if you cannot name the weakness a movement is for and show it moving, it is decoration, and decoration is what turns a focused program into a slog.

Use RPE honestly on grip work, too. A grip set should end because the grip is genuinely close to failing, not because the forearms burn. Burning is not failing, and stopping at the first burn leaves the adaptation on the table. On the other hand, grinding a grip set to a dropped weight every time beats up the tendons and teaches a sloppy release. Aim for one to two reps — or about five seconds — in reserve on most sets, and save the true-to-failure sets for once a week at most. Grip tendons recover slowly, and a program that ignores that fact ends in elbow pain, not stronger hands.

Deload grip work with everything else. When your program calls for a deload week, cut grip volume in half too, because the hands and forearms accumulate fatigue just like everything else. Lifters who deload their squat and keep hammering gripper work all week are surprised when the elbows still ache, but they should not be: the deload only works if the whole system gets lighter, hands included.

The mistakes that stall grip progress

Training grip only when it fails. Grip work done once, heroically, after a dropped deadlift, and then forgotten for a month, does nothing. Grip adapts to steady, boring frequency, and a single hard session mostly produces soreness. Put grip on the calendar the way you put squats on the calendar, and it will improve the way squats improve.

Using straps for everything, then blaming grip. Straps are a fine tool for letting the back and legs train past a grip limit on a given day, and there is no shame in them. But a lifter who straps every pull, every row, and every carry, and then wonders why grip never improves, is answering their own question. Use straps strategically — on the heaviest work sets where grip would cap the target muscles — and train grip separately, unstrapped, at the end. That split gives you both: unblocked main lifts and a grip that actually gets trained.

Chasing a gripper you cannot close. The gripper that sits on your desk, unclosed, for a year is not a goal. It is a paperweight. Buy the gripper you can close for reps, progress it, and move up when the reps come easily. The same rule applies to thick bars and pinch weights: the right load is the one you can hold with good form and progress, not the one that impresses someone watching.

Ignoring pain signals from the elbows and wrists. Forearm and elbow pain during grip work is not a badge of honor, and it is not something to train through. It is usually a sign of too much volume, too fast a progression, or a movement that does not fit your hands. Back the volume off by a third, drop the offending movement for two weeks, and come back lighter. A grip program that lasts a year beats a grip program that lasts three heroic weeks and then ends in a physio visit, every time.

Expecting grip to improve without tracking it. "My grip feels stronger" is not a measurement. Seconds held, meters carried, plates pinched, and gripper reps closed are measurements. Write them in the same logbook as your main lifts, review them monthly, and adjust the plan based on what moved and what did not. The lifter who tracks grip is the lifter whose grip actually gets stronger, because tracking turns a vague hope into a program.

How to test your grip

You do not need a dynamometer, though a cheap one is a fun way to get a number. Three simple tests tell you most of what you need to know. First, the dead hang: how long can you hang from a bar with a full grip before your fingers open? Under thirty seconds is a clear support-grip weakness. Second, the farmer carry: how far can you carry half your bodyweight in each hand before the grip opens? Under twenty meters is a clear sign to prioritize carries. Third, the plate pinch: can you hold two ten-pound plates smooth-side-out for thirty seconds in each hand? If not, pinch work earns a place in your program.

Retest every six to eight weeks, under the same conditions, and let the results steer the program. If the hang improves but the pinch does not, shift a set toward pinch work. If everything improves, keep the plan as it is — the most common grip programming error is changing a plan that is working because it feels too simple. Simple and working beats clever and unproven, in grip training as in everything else.

One honest caveat: grip numbers vary a lot by hand size, and small hands are not a sentence. A lifter with small hands will pinch less and hold a thick bar less easily, but support grip — the kind that matters most for barbell lifting — is trainable at any hand size, and technique (a proper hook grip, chalk, a bar that is not slick with sweat) closes much of the remaining gap. Train the grip you have, use the tools that fit your hands, and judge progress against your own numbers, not someone else's.

Putting it together

Start this week, at the end of your next session, with two movements: three sets of dead hangs and three sets of plate pinches. That is ten minutes. Add farmer carries to the next session, and gripper work to the one after that. Write down every number. In four weeks, compare the numbers, not the feeling, and adjust one thing — more time on the weakest test, or a little more weight on the strongest movement. In eight weeks, retest the three tests above. If the numbers moved, keep going. If they did not, the answer is almost never a new gadget. It is more consistency, a little more load, or a little less volume somewhere else so the hands can actually recover.

Grip strength is not a separate sport unless you want it to be. For most lifters, it is ten to fifteen minutes, two to three times a week, at the end of sessions that are already happening, in service of lifts that already matter. That is a small price for a ceiling removed — for deadlifts that end because the back is done, not because the hands opened, for carries that end because the legs quit, and for a pair of hands that hold on to whatever life asks them to hold. Train it like that: steadily, measurably, and last in the session, and it will stop being the thing that holds you back.
