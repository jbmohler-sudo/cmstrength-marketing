← The Strength Blog DEADLIFT PROGRAM · 8-WEEK PLAN Your Deadlift Is Stuck. The Program Is the Problem.

Most lifters do not have a deadlift problem. They have a deadlift program problem: random heavy singles one week, a forgotten accessory the next, and no plan for what happens when the weight stops moving. This guide builds an 8-week deadlift program from the ground up — the variation to train, the volume that works, the accessories that actually help, and a progression you can follow week to week.

Center Mass Strength

The deadlift is the simplest lift to program badly. It looks like it should be easy: pick the bar up, put it down, add weight next week. And for a while, that works. A beginner can add weight to the deadlift almost every session for months, and the simplicity feels like proof that the lift needs nothing more than effort. Then the easy gains end, and the same approach that built the first hundred pounds stops working entirely. The weight stalls, the back feels tired in a way it did not used to, and the lifter starts doing what most stalled lifters do: pulling heavy more often, chasing singles, and calling it intensity. That is not a program. That is a habit, and it is the habit that keeps most deadlifts stuck exactly where they are.

A real deadlift program is not complicated, but it is deliberate. It answers four questions before a single rep is pulled: which variation you are training, how much volume you are doing, how the weight progresses week to week, and what you do when a week goes wrong. Get those four right and the deadlift moves again, usually faster than expected, because the lift was never the problem. The plan around it was.

The short version

Most lifters deadlift too heavy, too often, with too little plan. The deadlift is the most fatiguing of the big lifts, and it is the one lifters are most tempted to max out every week, because a heavy single feels like progress even when it is not. A good program pulls heavy once a week, keeps the rest of the volume in the 70 to 85 percent range, and saves the true max for a test day that is planned weeks in advance. Frequency is low, intensity is managed, and the weight goes up on a schedule instead of on a feeling.

One main variation, trained consistently, beats a rotation of variations. Pick conventional or sumo based on your build and your goals, and train that variation as your main lift for the whole eight weeks. Other variations are accessories, not replacements: Romanian deadlifts, block pulls, and deficit pulls each fix a specific weakness, but none of them replaces the main lift. A lifter who changes variation every week is not training a deadlift. They are sampling deadlifts.

The 8-week structure is simple: build, then peak, then test. Weeks one through four build volume at moderate intensity. Weeks five and six push the top sets heavier. Week seven is a short, sharp peak. Week eight is a deload and a test. That shape works for almost everyone, because it respects the one fact that matters most about the deadlift: it takes longer to recover from than any other lift, and a program that ignores that fact will stall no matter how motivated the lifter is. Build, peak, test is one small macrocycle; the complete guide to powerlifting periodization and planning shows how cycles like this one nest inside a full meet prep.

Why most deadlift programs stall

The first reason deadlift programs stall is volume in the wrong place. A lifter who pulls heavy singles every session is doing the most fatiguing work possible with the least amount of practice. Singles build confidence and teach you to grind, but they do not build much strength per unit of fatigue, because one rep is one rep. The volume that builds a deadlift lives in sets of three to six, at weights you can move with good speed and good form, repeated week after week. The deadlift form guide covers the setup that keeps a bar moving well, and that setup is what lets you accumulate those reps safely. Form that falls apart at rep three is not a technique problem. It is a load problem, and a program that keeps the load honest is what fixes it.

The second reason is the absence of a progression rule. Most lifters add weight when they feel good and skip it when they do not, which means the program is being written by mood instead of by plan. A progression rule removes the decision: if you hit your target reps with bar speed intact, add five to ten pounds next week. If you missed reps, repeat the weight. If you missed twice, take ten percent off and rebuild. That is the whole rule, and it is boring on purpose. A program you have to think about every week is a program you will eventually stop following, and the lifters whose deadlifts keep moving are almost always the ones whose progression is too boring to argue with.

The third reason is accessory work that has nothing to do with the weakness. A lifter whose deadlift fails off the floor does not need more lockout work, and a lifter whose lockout fails does not need more deficit pulls. Accessories earn their place by fixing a specific sticking point, and the diagnostic is simple: where does the bar stop? Off the floor means leg drive and starting strength, which points to deficit pulls and paused deadlifts. At the knees means the transition, which points to block pulls and Romanian deadlifts. At lockout means the glutes and the upper back, which points to hip thrusts, rows, and carries. A program that throws every accessory at the wall is not thorough. It is unfocused, and unfocused accessory work is the most common way a deadlift program fills up with volume that does nothing.

The fourth reason is recovery, and it is the one lifters argue with most. The deadlift taxes the lower back, the grip, and the nervous system in a way no other lift does, and the cost does not show up the next day. It shows up two weeks later, as a top set that felt fine last month and feels glued to the floor now. The powerlifting program guide makes the same point about programming generally: the program has to fit the recovery you actually have, not the recovery you wish you had. One heavy deadlift day a week, with the rest of the week's pulling kept light and specific, is enough for almost everyone. Two heavy days is a young lifter's luxury, and even then it only works when the second day is a variation at a reduced load.

Pick your variation

Start with the stance question, because it decides everything else. Conventional and sumo are both legitimate deadlifts, and the right choice is mostly a matter of build. Long arms and a short torso usually suit conventional, because the bar does not have to travel far and the back can stay in a strong position. Short arms and long legs usually suit sumo, because the wide stance shortens the pull and lets the legs do more of the work. Neither is cheating, and neither is morally superior, whatever the internet says. The test is practical: pull both for a month, compare the numbers and how your back feels the day after, and pick the one that moves more weight with less cost. That is your main variation for this program, and it stays your main variation for all eight weeks.

Sumo deserves one extra note, because it is the variation lifters switch to and from most often. The sumo deadlift guide covers the stance and the hip position in detail, but the programming point is simple: sumo is less taxing on the lower back and more taxing on the hips, which means it often tolerates slightly more volume, but it also punishes a lifter who treats it like a wide-stance conventional pull. If you pick sumo, commit to the technique work. If you pick conventional, commit to the same. Half-committed is the only stance that never works.

Then pick one secondary variation, and only one, for the whole program. The choice follows the weakness. A lifter who is slow off the floor picks deficit deadlifts, standing on a one- to two-inch platform, for sets of five. A lifter who stalls at the knees picks block pulls, with the bar starting just below the knee, for sets of three to five. A lifter who cannot lock out picks Romanian deadlifts, which load the glutes and hamstrings through the exact range where the lockout happens. The Romanian deadlift form guide covers the hinge pattern that makes this movement work, and the hinge pattern is the whole point: a Romanian deadlift done like a stiff-legged row is not an accessory. It is a different exercise that does not transfer.

The trap bar deserves a mention for lifters whose backs are the limiter. A trap-bar deadlift is not a competition deadlift, but it is an excellent training tool: the load sits closer to your center of gravity, the back stays more upright, and most lifters can pull more weight with less spinal fatigue. If your program keeps stalling because your back never fully recovers, a block of trap-bar work as the secondary variation — or even as the main lift for one eight-week cycle — is a legitimate fix, not a cop-out. The goal is a bigger pull and a back that still works. The bar you use to get there is a detail.

How much deadlift volume actually works

For most lifters, the effective range is six to twelve hard sets of deadlift-pattern work per week, counting both the main lift and the secondary variation. Below six sets, there is not enough practice to drive adaptation. Above twelve, the fatigue starts costing more than the volume returns, and the back is usually the first thing to say so. Beginners sit at the low end of that range and progress fast on it. Intermediates sit in the middle. Advanced lifters can push toward the top, but only when the rest of their training is managed around it, because deadlift volume does not exist in a vacuum: heavy squats, rows, and carries all draw on the same recovery.

Intensity matters as much as volume. The main work sets should mostly live between 70 and 85 percent of your one-rep max, in sets of three to six reps. That range is heavy enough to build strength and light enough to move with speed, and speed is the tell: a deadlift set that grinds from the first rep is a set that is too heavy for the program that week. Singles above 90 percent have a place, but it is a small one — a top single every two to three weeks, or in the peak week, not every session. A lifter who tests every week is not training. They are just finding out, over and over, that they are tired.

Use RPE honestly on the deadlift, because percentages lie when recovery varies. A set of five at 80 percent should feel like an RPE 7 or 8 — hard, but with one or two reps left in the tank. If it feels like a 9 or 10, the weight is too heavy for the plan, and the right move is to repeat last week's weight rather than force the progression. The deadlift punishes ego more than any other lift, because the cost of a grinder set is paid in recovery over the next two weeks, not in soreness the next morning. The program works when the reps are fast and the bar speed is honest. It stops working the week the lifter starts pretending a grind is a training set.

One more volume rule, and it is the one that saves most programs: count the warm-up sets honestly. A lifter who works up to a top set of five at 400 pounds has already pulled hundreds of pounds of volume in the warm-up, and that volume counts. The warm-up should be just enough to prepare, not a second workout before the workout. Three to five warm-up sets, climbing in sensible jumps, and then the work sets. A warm-up that leaves you tired before the first work set is not a warm-up. It is the first mistake of the session.

The 8-week program

Here is the program, laid out week by week. It assumes one main deadlift day and one lighter secondary day, and it assumes you know your one-rep max or can estimate it honestly from a recent heavy set. If you do not know your max, test it first — a single heavy session, planned and recovered from, and then start the program the following week. Guessing your max and building a program on the guess is how lifters end up doing volume that is either too light to matter or too heavy to survive.

Weeks 1 to 2 — build the base. Main day: deadlift, 4 sets of 5 at 70 percent. Secondary day, two or three days later: Romanian deadlift, 3 sets of 8 at a moderate weight, plus a row variation for 3 sets of 8. The weights should feel comfortable, almost easy, and that is the point. These weeks build the habit and the volume base, and they let the back and grip adapt to the schedule before the loads get serious. A lifter who treats week one like a test has misunderstood the program: the test is eight weeks away, and everything before it is preparation.

Weeks 3 to 4 — add load. Main day: deadlift, 4 sets of 4 at 75 percent, then one back-off set of 6 at 65 percent. Secondary day: your chosen secondary variation — deficit pulls or block pulls — 3 sets of 5 at a weight that challenges the sticking point without breaking form, plus a carry for three trips of thirty to forty meters. The volume is similar to weeks one and two, but the intensity is higher, and the back-off set keeps the total reps up while the top sets get heavier. If a set slows to a grind, repeat the weight next week instead of adding. The program does not care about your calendar. It cares about your bar speed.

Weeks 5 to 6 — push the top sets. Main day: deadlift, work up to a top set of 3 at 82 to 85 percent, then 3 sets of 3 at 75 percent. Secondary day: Romanian deadlift, 3 sets of 6, heavier than weeks one and two, plus a row and a core movement. These are the hardest weeks of the program, and they are also the weeks where lifters most often add weight they have not earned. The rule stays the same: the top set should move with intent, not grind. A top triple that takes ten seconds is not a top triple. It is a warning.

Week 7 — peak. Main day: deadlift, work up to a single at 90 percent, then 2 sets of 3 at 75 percent. Secondary day: light Romanian deadlifts, 2 sets of 6, and nothing else heavy. This week is short on purpose. The single at 90 percent tells you where you are, and it tells you without the cost of a true max. If the single moves fast, the test next week will go well. If it grinds, the test will be honest about it, and that is useful information too.

Week 8 — deload and test. Monday or Tuesday: deadlift, 3 sets of 3 at 60 percent, easy, fast, done. Thursday or Friday, after at least two full rest days: test. Warm up carefully, take a single at 90 percent, then a single at your old max, then one attempt above it — five to ten pounds above, not twenty. If it moves, that is your new max, and the program worked. If it does not, you still learned something: either the volume was too much, the variation was wrong, or the recovery was short, and the next cycle adjusts one of those three things. Then take a deload week before starting the next cycle, because a test is a peak, and a peak is not a place to live.

After the test, run the program again with the new max, or switch the secondary variation to attack the next weakness. Most lifters get two or three good cycles out of this structure before they need anything more complicated, and the ones who do need more complicated programming are the ones who have already proven they can follow a simple one. Complexity is earned by consistency, not by impatience.

Accessories that actually help

The deadlift accessories that matter are the ones that load the muscles that fail, in the range where they fail. Rows build the upper back that keeps the bar close and the shoulders from rounding. Romanian deadlifts build the glutes and hamstrings through the lockout range. Carries build the grip and the core under a moving load, which is the closest thing to real-world strength a gym offers. Hip thrusts build glute lockout strength directly, and they do it without loading the spine, which makes them useful when the back is already carrying enough. The exercises guide covers how to pick assistance work for the weakness you actually have, and the same logic applies here: name the weakness first, then pick the movement. An accessory without a named weakness is a hobby.

Grip deserves its own line, because grip is the quiet limiter on more deadlift programs than lifters admit. A deadlift that slips out of the hands at lockout is not a back problem or a leg problem. It is a grip problem, and the fix is grip work at the end of sessions, not more deadlift volume. The grip strength guide covers the three kinds of grip and a weekly plan that fits around main lifts, and the dose is small: ten to fifteen minutes, two to three times a week, at the end. A lifter who straps every set and never trains grip will eventually discover that the grip they never trained is the thing standing between them and their next PR, and by then the fix takes weeks. Train it now, while it is cheap.

Core work belongs in a deadlift program too, but not the kind most lifters do. The deadlift needs a core that braces under load, which means planks, loaded carries, and heavy ab work — not endless crunches. Two or three sets of a bracing movement at the end of the deadlift day is enough, and the test is simple: can you brace hard enough that your midsection feels like a wall before the bar leaves the floor? If not, the core is the leak, and the leak is worth fixing before adding more weight to the bar.

What about the accessories that do not help? The ones that look like deadlifts but are not. Good mornings are a fine exercise, but they are not a deadlift accessory for most lifters — they load the same back that is already tired from the main lift, without loading the pull itself. Leg press is a fine exercise, but it does not teach the hinge, and a lifter whose deadlift fails off the floor needs hinge practice, not more quad volume. The test for any accessory is whether it loads the failing muscle in the failing range. If it does not, it is general training, and general training belongs in the program as general training, not dressed up as a deadlift fix.

Progression rules and what to do when you miss

The progression rule for the main lift is simple: add five to ten pounds per week to the work sets, as long as every set moved with bar speed and the last rep looked like the first. Five pounds for most lifters, ten for a beginner or a lifter coming back from a layoff. That is the whole rule, and it works because the deadlift responds to steady loading better than to heroic jumps. A lifter who adds ten pounds a week for eight weeks has added eighty pounds to their work sets, and a lifter who adds twenty pounds one week and then stalls for three has added twenty. Slow and steady is not a platitude here. It is arithmetic.

When you miss, the response depends on how you missed. Missed reps because the bar slowed to a grind but you finished the set: repeat the weight next week, and focus on bar speed. Missed reps because you could not complete the set: drop ten percent next week and rebuild from there. Missed two weeks in a row: the program is telling you something, and the answer is almost always one of three things — the volume is too high, the secondary variation is wrong, or life outside the gym is eating your recovery. Change one thing, not all three, and give it two weeks before judging. A program changed every week is not a program. It is a series of guesses.

The most common miss is not a missed rep. It is a missed session, and the rule for a missed session is to skip it, not to make it up. A deadlift day crammed into the day before the next deadlift day is not recovery. It is two deadlift days in a row, and the back will notice. The beginner program guide makes the same point for new lifters: the program survives a missed session easily, and it does not survive a lifter who tries to be perfect by doubling up. Miss a day, move on, and hit the next one.

The mistakes that stall deadlift progress

Maxing every week. A max is a test, and a test repeated weekly stops being a test. It becomes the training, and training made of maxes is training made of fatigue. The deadlift responds to volume at moderate intensity, not to a weekly referendum on how strong you are. Test every eight weeks. Train in between.

Deadlifting heavy twice a week. Two heavy deadlift days look like dedication and feel like progress, until the back stops recovering and the numbers go backwards. One heavy day and one light secondary day is the structure that works for almost everyone. The lifters who can handle two heavy days are the exception, and they are usually young, well-fed, and not squatting heavy in the same week. If your squat is also heavy, one deadlift day is not a compromise. It is the program.

Ignoring the back until it forces a break. Lower-back tightness that lingers past the next day is not normal training soreness. It is a signal, and the right response is to cut the volume by a third for a week and see if it clears. Lifters who train through it do not get tougher. They get injured, and then they get a forced layoff that costs more than the deload week ever would have. The deadlift rewards patience and punishes stubbornness more than any other lift, because the thing that breaks is the thing you need for everything else.

Changing the program every three weeks. A program needs eight weeks to show what it can do, and a lifter who changes it at week three has not tested a program. They have tested three weeks of a program and then quit. The deadlift is a slow lift. It moves in five-pound jumps and eight-week cycles, and the lifters who accept that pace are the ones whose deadlifts keep moving year after year. The lifters who do not accept it are the ones still looking for a program, years later, with the same deadlift they started with.

Treating straps as a moral issue. Straps are a tool. Use them on the heaviest work sets when grip would cap the back and legs, and train grip separately. Do not use them on everything, and do not refuse them on principle. A lifter who will not use straps and whose grip limits every top set is not being tough. They are training their grip and calling it a deadlift program, and the deadlift is the thing not getting trained.

Putting it together

Start this week. Pick your variation, conventional or sumo, and commit to it for eight weeks. Set your main day and your secondary day, write down the weights from the plan above, and pull the first session at 70 percent for 4 sets of 5. It will feel light, and that is correct. The program is not asking you to prove anything in week one. It is asking you to show up, pull with speed, and come back next week for a little more. That is how deadlifts get built: not in a single heroic session, but in eight weeks of sessions that each did what they were supposed to do.

Write down every number. The top set weight, the bar speed, the reps completed, and how the back felt the next day. In four weeks, compare the numbers, not the feeling, and adjust one thing if something is not moving. In eight weeks, test, and let the test tell you what the next cycle should be. If the numbers moved, the program worked, and the next cycle starts from a stronger place. If they did not, the answer is in the log: the volume, the variation, or the recovery, and the fix is one change, not a new program.

The deadlift is the most honest lift in the gym. It does not care about your plan, your effort, or your enthusiasm. It cares about whether you pulled the weight, with good form, on the schedule you set. A program that respects that honesty — one variation, the right volume, a boring progression, and a test every eight weeks — is a program that works. Run it, write it down, and let the bar tell you the truth.
