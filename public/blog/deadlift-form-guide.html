Deadlift Form, Done Right.

The deadlift punishes a bad setup and rewards a good one — and the setup is decided before the bar leaves the floor. The five-step setup, both stances compared honestly, the five mistakes everyone makes, and how to program it for decades of heavy pulls.

Center Mass Strength

The squat and the bench forgive. You can set up sloppy, feel something wrong halfway down, and still salvage the rep. The deadlift offers no such grace period. There is no descent to think through, no stretch reflex to bail you out — just you, the bar, and a position you either built before the pull or didn't. Every ugly deadlift you've ever seen was decided in the three seconds before the bar moved. Every clean one too.

That's why deadlift form is the highest-leverage technique work in the gym. A lifter who adds five pounds to his squat setup adds five pounds to the lift. A lifter who fixes his deadlift setup can add twenty overnight — same muscles, same day, because the old setup was bleeding force into a bar path that curved around his knees. This guide is the whole thing: why the setup is the rep, the five steps that build it, conventional versus sumo with no tribalism, grip choices, the five mistakes everyone makes, and programming that keeps the deadlift moving for decades instead of grinding it into the ground by spring.

The squat has a descent where you can think. The deadlift has a setup where you must.

What this guide covers: why the setup decides the rep before the bar moves, the five-step setup (bar position, shin contact, brace, wedge, leg drive), conventional vs. sumo honestly compared, grip choices (double overhand, mixed, hook), the five universal mistakes with fixes, and programming: frequency, RPE ceilings, variation work, and where the deadlift lives in the macrocycle. Why the Setup Is the Rep

A deadlift has exactly one eccentric phase per set, and it happens after the rep is already over. That makes the deadlift unique among the big three: the concentric is the entire lift, which means there is no rhythm to find, no bounce to borrow, no reversal to time. The only thing you control is the starting position. So the starting position has to be right every time — not approximately right, not right on rep one and decaying by rep five.

This is also why the deadlift is the lift most people "feel in their back." It's not because deadlifting is inherently bad for backs. It's because a bad setup turns the lift into a back exercise: hips too high and the back takes the whole pull; bar too far forward and the lumbar spine works as a lever arm instead of a strut. Done right, the deadlift is a full-body pull where the legs push, the lats hold the bar close, and the trunk is a rigid transmitter of force. The difference between those two lifts isn't the weight on the bar. It's three feet of floor space and a checklist.

One more thing the setup decides: the bar path. A deadlifted bar must travel in a straight vertical line over the mid-foot — the shortest distance and the only one the physics allows at heavy weights. Every setup error bends that path: bar forward of the foot and the rep swings out and back; hips too low and the knees shove the bar forward on the way up. Fix the setup and the path fixes itself. Chase the path and you'll never find the setup.

Conventional vs. Sumo, Honestly

Most of the internet treats this like a religion. It's a geometry problem. Both stances pull the same weight from the same floor to the same lockout; they just distribute the work across different joints. Your build decides which one is natural, and the honest test is a training block of each — not a debate in the comments.

ConventionalSumo
Feet hip-width, hands outside the kneesFeet wide, hands inside the knees
Longer range of motion, more back angleShorter range of motion, more upright torso
Back, glutes, and hamstrings do the heavy lifting off the floorQuads and adductors share more of the floor; hips finish it
Natural for long arms relative to torso — the bar reaches the hips without extreme hip mobilityNatural for shorter arms and wide hips — the stance puts you closer to the bar at the start
The classical pull; every lifter should learn it firstDemands real hip mobility and patience; looks easy, isn't

The practical read: learn conventional first, because it's the foundation everything else is measured against and the stance you'll default to in a strongman or general-strength context. Then give sumo an honest 6–8 week block. Some lifters gain 10–15 percent immediately because their proportions were always sumo-shaped and nobody told them. Others find sumo feels like folding themselves into a suitcase and go right back to conventional with better information.

And the trap bar deserves one line: it's a fine deadlift variation and a genuinely good main deadlift for masters lifters and general athletes — more upright, more quad, easier on the back — but it isn't a powerlifting deadlift. If you compete, the straight bar is the lift. If you don't, the trap bar is allowed to be the answer.

The Setup, Step by Step

Do this identically on every rep, light or heavy. The point of a setup routine isn't superstition — it's that the heavy single on the platform uses the same nervous-system pattern as the warmup with 135, and the only way that happens is if there is no difference between them.

  • Bar over mid-foot. Stand with the bar over the middle of your whole foot — not the toes, not the ankle, the middle. For most lifters this puts the bar about an inch in front of the shins. This is the single most common setup error in commercial gyms: the bar starts six inches out and the entire rep is a rescue mission.
  • Shins to the bar, chest up. Without moving the bar, hinge down and bring your shins forward until they touch it. Then lift your chest — this is the cue that sets your back angle. Do NOT move the bar when your shins come in; the bar stays over mid-foot. Your hips will land wherever they land. That's fine. The back is set by the chest, not by the hips.
  • Grip it and pull the slack out. Take your grip — double overhand for warmups, your working grip for work sets — and then pull the bar up just enough to hear it click against the plates. This is the most skipped step in deadlifting and the difference between a smooth pull and a yank. Slack in the system becomes a shockwave when the plates leave the floor.
  • Brace and wedge. Big breath into the belly, ribs down, trunk locked. Then "wedge" — drive your chest up and your hips toward the bar without letting the bar move or your hips rise. You're taking tension into the hamstrings and lats before the pull starts. The rep should feel like you're pulling yourself down into the bar, not lifting the bar off the floor.
  • Push the floor away. The cue off the floor is legs, not back — think leg press, not back extension. The bar drags up the shins and thighs in that straight vertical line because your lats are holding it there. Finish by driving the hips through to lockout: stand tall, glutes on, no lean-back. The lockout is a standing plank, not a limbo.

Film your heavy sets from the side, bar height visible, floor to lockout in frame. The checklist above is a felt sense; the video is the measurement. Most lifters who "pull the slack out" are yanking, and most lifters who think the bar is over mid-foot have it four inches forward. The camera doesn't argue.

The Five Mistakes Everyone Makes

Same five, same gyms, same order of frequency. The fixes are mechanical, not motivational:

  • The back rounds as the bar breaks the floor. The number-one deadlift failure, and it's a setup problem: hips too high, chest never lifted, slack never pulled. Fix: reset and rebuild. Lower the weight until the back you set is the back you finish with, then build from there. If your back changes shape mid-rep, the weight exceeded your setup — full stop.
  • The hips shoot up before the bar moves. The classic stripper pull: you set a decent position, then the hips rise and the back flattens out horizontal before the plates budge. Fix: wedge harder in step four and think "chest up" as the bar breaks. Paused deadlifts — pause an inch off the floor — teach the legs to stay in the pull instead of handing it to the back.
  • The bar drifts forward around the knees. Either the bar started too far out (step one failed) or the lats went slack and let it swing. Fix: "lats on" is a real cue — think of bending the bar around your legs, or squeezing oranges in your armpits. The bar should leave chalk on your shins and thighs. If your shins are clean, the bar was never close.
  • Yanking instead of wedging. The rep starts with a violent jerk — the lifter's version of revving an engine with no oil. The yank shocks the system, the back rounds to absorb it, and the lockout becomes a separate exercise. Fix: pull the slack out in step three and treat the first inch as slow. Heavy deadlifts are patient off the floor and violent at the top, not the reverse.
  • Hyperextending the lockout. Leaning back at the top to "finish" the rep, turning the lockout into a lumbar extension under load. Fix: the finish is glutes squeezing you upright, ribs down, standing tall. If you're leaning back, your glutes didn't finish the job — which usually means they're weak relative to your pull, and hip thrusts or block pulls belong in your program.

Notice the pattern: four of the five are setup errors, and the fifth is a glute-strength error. Nobody's deadlift is limited by a lack of effort. They're limited by positions built in a hurry.

Three Ways to Hold It

Grip is the deadlift's quiet limiter. The back and legs can almost always pull more than the hands can hold, and how you solve that decides what fails first:

GripReality
Double overhand. Both palms facing you.The honest grip and the best teacher — when it fails, your grip is the limiter, and that's information. Use it for every warmup and every rep you can. It builds the grip it tests.
Mixed grip. One palm in, one palm out.The classic heavy grip: the bar can't roll both ways. But the supinated arm's bicep is in the line of fire — a torn bicep on a heavy deadlift is almost always the mixed-grip arm. Keep that arm straight as a rope, and alternate which hand supinates to avoid building a twist.
Hook grip. Thumb trapped under the fingers, both palms in.The powerlifter's answer: as secure as mixed with none of the asymmetry. It hurts for the first month — genuinely, skin-peelingly hurts — and then the thumbs adapt and it becomes the strongest grip you own. Tape the thumbs and commit.

The system that works: double overhand until it fails, hook grip for the work sets once you're serious, mixed as the bridge or the permanent choice if your thumbs veto the hook. Straps are for the top sets and rep work where the goal is back-and-legs volume, not a grip test — pair them with direct grip work (double-overhand holds, farmer's carries) and the grip keeps pace with the pull.

How to Program It

The deadlift is the lift people over-program. It's the heaviest thing in the gym, the most systemically demanding, and the slowest to recover from — and lifters treat it like a squat that starts from the floor. It's not. Respect the recovery cost and the deadlift pays you back for decades. Ignore it and the deadlift is the first lift to stall and the last to move again.

Frequency. One true heavy day a week is the standard for a reason. A second day is fine if it's a variation, not a second heavy day: Romanian deadlifts, deficits, tempo work, or block pulls at moderate loads. The variations build the competition pull without charging the full recovery price. And on the weeks the body says no — short sleep, heavy squats yesterday — the deadlift is the lift to autoregulate down, not the lift to prove something on. It remembers abuse longer than any other lift.

Sets, reps, and effort. The deadlift lives in the 1–6 rep range. Heavy work means low reps: singles, doubles, and triples at RPE 7–9. Sets of 8–10 deadlifts are a conditioning exercise wearing a strength costume — form decays, the back pays, and nobody's pull got strong on high-rep deadlift sets. If RPE as a language is new to you, the deadlift is where it matters most: the difference between RPE 8 and RPE 10 on a deadlift is the difference between a training stimulus and a recovery crater. Volume comes from sets, not reps: 3–6 sets of 2–5 will build any deadlift ever built.

  • Standard heavy work: 3–5 sets of 2–5 reps at RPE 7–9, once a week. This is the deadlift program. Everything else is seasoning.
  • Deficit deadlifts: stand on 1–3 inches of plates. The floor-strength builder — longer pull, more leg drive. Use when you miss off the floor.
  • Paused deadlifts: pause an inch off the floor or just below the knee. The position builder — exposes exactly where your setup breaks.
  • Block / rack pulls: bar elevated to knee or just below. The lockout builder — overloads the top half and the glutes that finish it.
  • Romanian deadlifts: the hinge pattern under control, constant tension. The hamstring and back-position builder — and the best deadlift accessory most lifters skip.

Where it lives in the macrocycle. The deadlift is a peaking lift: it sharpens fast and fades slow, which means it belongs heavy in the strength block and the peak, and deliberately light or absent in the taper. The full logic of lift placement across blocks is the periodization guide's territory. The short version for the deadlift: build it with variations in the accumulation block, test it in the strength block, sharpen it in the peak, and on meet week it barely exists. The lift you pull on the platform was built months ago.

If your deadlift has been stuck, diagnose the failure point before changing anything: floor, knees, or lockout. Each one has a variation with its name on it, and guessing is how lifters spend a year doing block pulls when their problem was the setup off the floor. The video from the setup section is the diagnostic tool. Watch where the bar stops, and program for that.

Deadlift Form After 40

The deadlift after 40 is the same lift with a different recovery clock. The positions don't change — bar over mid-foot, flat back, straight path — but the programming around them does, because a 45-year-old nervous system takes longer to clear a heavy deadlift session than a 25-year-old's, and pretending otherwise is how backs get "tweaked" for six months.

Three practical adjustments. First, frequency: heavy deadlifts every 10–14 days is plenty for most masters lifters, with a variation (Romanian, trap bar, tempo) filling the weeks between. The total doesn't care whether the heavy day was Tuesday or the Tuesday before last. Second, the RPE ceiling: cap heavy work at RPE 8 most of the year. The deadlift's last rep is the one that costs the most and teaches the least; masters lifters should rarely visit it. Third, the trap bar: if the straight-bar setup is fighting your hips or your back, the trap bar is not quitting — it's a deadlift with a shorter moment arm and a more upright torso, which is exactly the modification an aging spine appreciates. Train the pattern, not the implement.

One specific caution for the mixed grip crowd: the supinated-arm bicep tear is a masters-lifter injury with depressing regularity. If you've pulled mixed for years and the elbows are getting cranky, this is the decade to learn the hook grip or reach for the straps on top sets. The pull is the point; the grip style is negotiable.

Deadlift Form: Common Questions

Should I deadlift conventional or sumo?

Whichever lets you keep a flat back and pull in a straight vertical line. Sumo shortens the range of motion and leans more on the quads and adductors; conventional loads the back and hamstrings harder. Build is the deciding factor: long arms relative to torso usually suit conventional, shorter arms and wide hips often suit sumo. Try both seriously for a training block before committing — the answer is in the training log, not the comments.

Is a rounded back on the deadlift always bad?

Upper-back rounding that doesn't change under load is a technique some advanced lifters use intentionally; lumbar rounding that happens as the weight breaks the floor is a setup failure. For everyone who isn't an advanced competitor chasing a max: the back you set before the pull is the back you finish with. If it changes shape mid-rep, the weight was too heavy for your setup.

When should I start using a belt on the deadlift?

When your brace is the limiter rather than your technique — typically once sets above about 80 percent of your max are a regular thing. A belt doesn't protect your back by itself; it gives your braced trunk something to push against so you can create more intra-abdominal pressure. Learn to brace hard without it first, then add the belt on your heavy sets.

Will using straps ruin my grip strength?

Not if you use them the way strong lifters do: straps on heavy working sets and rep-outs, bare hands on warmups and grip work. Your deadlift is a back-and-legs exercise, not a forearm exercise — letting the grip fail first is leaving pounds on the floor. Train the grip directly (double-overhand holds, farmer's carries) and it keeps pace.

How often should I deadlift heavy?

Less often than you want to. One true heavy day a week is enough for most lifters, with a lighter variation (Romanian deadlift, deficit, or tempo work) on a second day if recovery allows. Masters lifters often do better deadlifting heavy every 10 to 14 days and using variations in between. The deadlift is the most systemically demanding lift in the gym; program it like it is.

Why do I always miss at the lockout / off the floor?

Missing off the floor usually means your setup broke down — hips too high, no slack pulled out, or you're yanking instead of wedging. Missing at lockout usually means your glutes aren't finishing: the bar stalls at the knees because the hips never drove through. Deficit pulls fix the floor; paused and block pulls fix the lockout. The fix matches the failure point.
