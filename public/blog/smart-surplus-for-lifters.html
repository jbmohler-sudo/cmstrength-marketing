The Smart Surplus.

How to eat to build muscle without the sloppy bulk — surplus size, protein, and the brakes that keep it clean.

Center Mass Strength

There are two ways lifters eat to grow. One is the smart surplus — enough extra food to build muscle, tracked well enough that the fat gain stays in check. The other is the excuse to eat everything in sight for six months and call it bulking, then spend the next six months dieting off what you never needed to gain. This post is about the first one.

Growing takes food. You can’t build significant muscle out of thin air, and the lifter eating at maintenance for a year while wondering why nothing changed is leaving gains on the table. But more food isn’t automatically better. The whole game of a smart surplus is eating just enough extra.

How Big the Surplus Should Be

The target is small: roughly 200–300 calories above maintenance per day. That’s it. Not 1,000. Not “eat everything.” A couple hundred extra calories is the difference between a body with enough raw material to grow and one spinning its wheels — and it’s the difference between gaining mostly muscle and gaining mostly fat.

Here’s why the number matters. Muscle grows slowly — even in a good year, a natural lifter might add a few pounds of actual muscle. Fat, on the other hand, goes on as fast as you feed it. Push the surplus to 800+ calories and the extra 500 above what your body can use for muscle goes straight to fat. You’re not building faster. You’re just getting fatter faster.

The bodyweight target: aim for roughly 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight gained per week. For a 200-lb lifter, that’s about half a pound to a pound per week. Faster than that and you’re outgrowing your own muscle-building capacity. Slower than that and you might as well be at maintenance.

For beginners and returning lifters, the higher end of that range is fine — your body is more responsive, and there’s more room for error. For experienced lifters who’ve been at it for years, stay on the lower end. Your margins are thinner.

Protein Still Runs the Show

A surplus doesn’t change the protein math — it just makes hitting it easier because you’re eating more food overall. Stay in the 1.6–2.2 g/kg per day range. The surplus calories on top of that should come mostly from carbs, since carbs fuel training, and training is what actually builds the muscle.

Fat intake doesn’t need to go crazy either. Enough for health and hormones — roughly 0.7–1.0 g/kg — and the rest is carbs. A lifter eating 3,500 calories a day with 90 g of fat and 400+ g of carbs will train circles around one eating 3,500 calories of bacon and butter.

What “Sloppy” Actually Looks Like

Here’s the trap: the first month of a surplus feels great. The scale climbs, the lifts move, you feel full and strong. So you keep eating the same way, or a little more, because it’s working. By month four you’ve gained 15 pounds, half of it fat, your belt is tight, your conditioning is gone, and now you need a cut to fix a problem you created.

Sloppy isn’t the surplus. Sloppy is the surplus with no brakes. The brakes are the weekly check-in:

  • Bodyweight trend: Weigh daily, watch the weekly average. If the average gain is running ahead of your 0.25–0.5% target for two weeks straight, pull the surplus back a couple hundred calories. If it’s flat for two weeks, add a couple hundred.
  • The waistline: This is your early-warning system. If your waist is climbing faster than your lifts, the surplus is feeding fat. A small increase is normal — a runaway one isn’t.
  • The lifts: In a proper surplus, training should feel good. You’re fueled. If your lifts are stalling while you’re eating more, the problem is the program, not the food — adding more calories won’t fix a training problem.

Adjust in small steps, not swings. Two hundred calories up or down, hold for two weeks, look at the trend. That’s the whole control system.

The Minimums That Keep It Clean

A smart surplus is boring, and boring is what works. A few rules that keep the whole thing on rails:

Eat like a lifter, not like it’s your last meal. Most of your food should still be real food — the meals you’d eat at maintenance, just slightly bigger or with one more meal in the day. “Bulking” is not permission to live on fast food. The quality of the surplus affects how you feel and train, even if the calories are the same.

Keep training hard and keep the conditioning in. The surplus makes the heavy work feel good — use it. Don’t let conditioning slide to zero either; a couple of easy sessions a week keeps work capacity up so the extra bodyweight doesn’t turn you into a lifter who gets winded warming up.

Sleep. Growth happens when you recover, and recovery happens when you sleep. A 400-calorie surplus with 5 hours of sleep builds less muscle than a 200-calorie surplus with 8 hours. Nobody wants to hear it, but it’s true.

Don’t let it run forever. A building phase has a natural length — usually 4 to 6 months before the fat gain starts outpacing the muscle gain and you need to tighten up. Set the length up front. “Bulk until I’m fat” is how people bulk for two years.

The Takeaway

Eating to build muscle is the simplest phase of nutrition there is: eat a couple hundred calories more than you burn, keep protein high, let carbs fuel the training, and watch the scale and the waist like a hawk. The surplus is a tool, not a lifestyle — small enough to stay lean, controlled enough to know when to adjust, and ended on a schedule instead of when your pants stop fitting. Do that, and you come out of a building phase bigger and still looking like a lifter, instead of spending the next six months undoing your own work.
