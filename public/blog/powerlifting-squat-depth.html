← The Strength Blog SQUAT DEPTH · COMPETITION STANDARD How Low Is Low Enough? Hit Depth Every Time.

Squat depth is the one call that can erase your best lift in a second. This guide covers the competition depth rule, how to test your own depth honestly, and the mobility and technique fixes that get you below parallel — and keep you there under heavy weight.

Center Mass Strength

Every powerlifter has watched it happen: a squat that looked strong, moved fast, and came back up clean — and then two red lights. The lift was not too heavy. The lifter was not too weak. The squat was two inches high, and two inches is the whole sport. Depth is the cheapest way to lose a lift you were strong enough to make, and it is also the most fixable problem in powerlifting, because the rule is clear, the test is simple, and the fixes are mostly unglamorous work that any lifter can do. The lifters who never worry about depth are not the ones with the best mobility. They are the ones who built depth into their training on purpose, long before they ever stepped on a platform.

Depth is not a talent. It is a skill with a standard, and the standard does not move. The hip crease has to drop below the top of the knee. That is the rule in every federation that matters, and it is judged from the side by people who have watched thousands of squats. Your training depth has to be deeper than your competition depth, because the depth you hit in a quiet gym with a light bar is not the depth you will hit on a platform with a max on your back, adrenaline in your system, and a crowd watching. Train an inch below parallel, and the meet will give you parallel. Train exactly to parallel, and the meet will take it away.

The short version

The rule is the hip crease below the top of the knee. Not the thigh, not the shorts, not the feeling of being low — the top surface of the leg at the hip joint has to pass below the top of the knee joint, judged from the side. That is the whole standard, and it is the same whether you squat high bar or low bar, wide or narrow, raw or equipped. Video from the side is the only honest test. A mirror lies, a training partner guessing from the front lies worse, and your own feeling of depth is the least reliable measure of all, because the bottom of a heavy squat feels much lower than it looks. Film every heavy set from the side, at hip height, and judge the frame where you are lowest. Most depth problems are not mobility problems. They are stance problems, bar-position problems, and bracing problems — and the lifters who fix depth fastest are the ones who test those three before they spend a month stretching their ankles.

Depth is trained, not hoped for. Pause squats, tempo squats, and honest video checks build depth the same way any other quality is built: by practicing the exact thing, at a load you can control, until it is automatic. A lifter who only ever squats to depth when the weight is light does not have depth. They have a light squat. Depth counts when it survives the top set.

The rule: what counts as depth

The rule, as written in the IPF technical rules and mirrored by the USAPL and most other federations, is that the lifter must bend the knees and lower the body until the top surface of the legs at the hip joint is lower than the top of the knees. In plain language: the crease where your thigh meets your hip has to drop below the top of your knee. The side judge makes the call, because only the side view shows the relationship between hip and knee. The front judge cannot see it, and the head judge is watching the start and the rack commands. Two of the three judges have to give a white light, so the side judge's call decides most depth verdicts by itself.

That standard has two consequences lifters forget. First, depth is a relationship between two points on your body, not a distance from the floor. A long-legged lifter and a short-legged lifter both have to hit the same relationship, but the long-legged lifter travels much further to get there, which is why femur length matters so much to squat depth and why lifters with long legs need wider stances or more forward knee travel to hit the same standard. Second, the rule does not reward going deeper than required. There is no bonus for ass-to-grass. A squat that is three inches below parallel counts exactly the same as a squat that is half an inch below parallel, and the extra three inches cost energy, position, and bar speed on the way back up. The goal is not maximum depth. The goal is reliable depth, with a small margin, every single rep.

Different federations word the rule the same way, but judging strictness varies, and that variation is part of the sport. Some meets are called tight, some loose, and a lifter who trains to the exact line is gambling on the judging panel. The fix is a margin: train a full inch below parallel, so that even a strict panel sees white lights, and so that the depth you lose under meet-day adrenaline still leaves you legal. The complete guide to prepping for your first powerlifting meet makes the same point about attempt selection — the meet rewards the lifter who builds in margin everywhere, and depth is the first place to build it.

Why lifters miss depth

The most common reason lifters miss depth is that they never actually trained it. They squatted to a depth that felt low, nobody corrected them, and the habit set in over months. Then they filmed a heavy set, or they did a mock meet, or they bombed out of a real one, and discovered that their comfortable squat was an inch high. The feeling of depth is unreliable because the bottom of a squat is the most stressful position in the lift: the muscles are stretched, the load is heavy, and the brain's priority is getting back up, not measuring joint angles. A squat that feels deep is usually a squat that stopped at the first point of discomfort, which is almost always above parallel.

The second reason is the weight itself. Depth is the first thing that goes when the load climbs past what the lifter can control, because cutting depth is the body's cheapest way to make a heavy squat easier. Shorten the range of motion and the lift gets lighter — that is physics, and every lifter's body knows it. This is why depth has to be trained at heavy loads, not just light ones. A lifter who hits depth on warm-ups and cuts it on top sets does not have a depth problem in the warm-up. They have a depth problem where it counts, and the fix is to take weight off the bar until depth survives the top set, then build back up without losing it. That feels like going backwards. It is the fastest way forwards.

The third reason is bracing. A lifter who loses their brace on the way down loses depth with it, because the torso folds, the hips shoot back, and the descent stops early to protect the position. Depth requires a brace that holds all the way down and all the way back up, and the bottom position is where the brace is tested hardest. If your squats get high exactly when they get heavy, check your brace before you check your ankles. The complete barbell squat form and programming guide covers the bracing sequence step by step, and the depth section of this guide assumes that sequence is already in place.

The fourth reason is the stance, and it is the one lifters change last because they are attached to the stance they have. A stance that is too narrow for your hip structure will not let you drop below parallel no matter how mobile you are, because the femurs run into the pelvis before the hips can descend. A stance that is too wide will not let you drive out of the hole. The right stance is the one that lets you hit depth with the knees tracking over the toes and the torso staying upright enough to drive. That stance is different for every lifter, and finding it is a matter of testing widths in the gym, not copying a favorite lifter's foot placement.

How to test your own depth

Film from the side. That is the whole method, and everything else is a refinement. Set the phone at hip height, about eight to ten feet away, directly side-on to the lifter, and film the whole set. Then watch the frame where the lifter is at their lowest, and draw the line in your head: is the hip crease below the top of the knee? If you cannot tell from the video, the answer is probably no — depth that is clearly legal is obvious on film, and depth that needs a debate is depth that will get red lights from a strict judge. Film heavy sets, not just warm-ups, because heavy sets are where depth disappears. Film from the same side and the same distance every time, so you can compare week to week.

The mirror is a bad test. A side mirror shows you a distorted view of your own depth, and a front mirror shows you nothing useful at all — depth is judged from the side, and the front view hides the hip-knee relationship entirely. A training partner calling depth is better than a mirror but still unreliable unless they are standing in the side judge's position and know exactly what to look for. A partner standing in front of you and saying "that was deep" is guessing, and the guess is usually generous. The video does not guess. It shows the frame, and the frame is the verdict.

There are two other useful tests. The first is the box test: set a box at the height where your hip crease is just below your knee — measure it, do not guess — and squat to a light touch on the box. If you can touch the box with control and stand back up without collapsing onto it, your depth is real. If you cannot reach it, you have found the gap. The second is the pause test: pause for two seconds at the bottom of a light squat and check whether you are still below parallel. A pause removes momentum, and momentum is what hides a high squat — the bounce out of the hole can make a high squat feel deep, because the turnaround is fast and the effort is high. A two-second pause takes the bounce away and leaves only the position. If the position is high, the pause will tell you.

The mobility limiters — and the ones that are not

Ankle mobility is the limiter lifters blame most, and it is a real one. The squat needs the knees to travel forward over the toes, and the ankles have to dorsiflex to allow that. If your ankles are stiff, the knees cannot travel, the hips cannot descend, and the squat stops high — or the heels lift, which is the same problem wearing a different costume. Test it: stand facing a wall with your toes four inches from the wall and try to touch your knee to the wall without your heel lifting. If you cannot, ankle mobility is part of your depth problem. The fix is a mix of daily calf and ankle work and, more immediately, a heeled lifting shoe. A heel lift of about three-quarters of an inch effectively gives your ankles the range they do not have, and it is the single fastest fix for a lifter whose depth is limited by ankle stiffness. It is not cheating. It is equipment, the same as a belt.

Hip mobility is the second limiter, and it is more structural. The hips have to flex deeply while the femurs rotate outward, and the shape of your hip socket decides how far that can go. Some lifters can sit into a deep squat with a narrow stance and no effort. Others need a wider stance and more toe-out to find the same depth, because their hip structure closes the joint earlier. This is not a flexibility problem you can stretch away in a month. It is anatomy, and the answer is to find the stance that works with your hips instead of fighting them. Stand in your squat stance, squat down without a bar, and feel where the hips stop. Then widen the stance an inch, turn the toes out five degrees, and try again. The stance that lets you drop lowest with the knees tracking over the toes is the stance your hips want.

Femur length is the limiter nobody can change. Long femurs mean a longer lever, a longer trip to parallel, and a torso that has to lean further forward to keep the bar over the mid-foot. Long-legged lifters need more forward knee travel, more ankle mobility, and usually a wider stance to hit depth without folding in half. Short-legged lifters have the opposite problem: depth comes easily, but the squat can get so deep that they lose tension at the bottom and struggle to drive out. Neither build is better. Each one needs a different setup, and the lifters who struggle most with depth are usually the ones who copied a setup built for the opposite body.

But here is the honest part: for most lifters, mobility is not the limiter at all. The limiter is the stance, the bar position, or the willingness to take weight off the bar and rebuild. Before you spend a month on ankle mobility, test the cheap fixes. Widen your stance. Turn your toes out. Try a heeled shoe. Drop ten percent off the bar and see if depth appears. If depth appears when the weight drops, it was never a mobility problem. It was a load problem, and no amount of stretching fixes a load problem.

Stance and bar position

Stance width is the biggest lever on depth, and it is the one most lifters set once and never revisit. The test is simple: with an empty bar, squat to depth at your current stance, then move each foot out an inch and try again, then another inch. At some width, depth gets easier — the hips open up, the torso stays more upright, and the bottom feels like a position you can drive out of instead of a place you fall into. Past that width, depth gets harder again, because the adductors are stretched too far and the drive out of the hole weakens. The right width is the one in the middle, and it is usually wider than the stance a lifter starts with, because most lifters start too narrow. Take the time to find it. A stance change costs a week of awkward squats and pays for itself for years.

Toe angle matters as much as width. Toes pointed straight ahead force the knees to track straight ahead, which closes the hips early for most lifters. Toes turned out fifteen to thirty degrees let the knees track out over the toes, which opens the hips and lets the pelvis drop between the femurs instead of running into them. The knees should follow the toes — if the toes point out and the knees cave in, the depth you gain is not worth the knee position you lose. Set the feet, then squat, and watch the knees. They should track in the same direction as the toes for the whole descent.

Bar position changes the depth equation too. A high-bar squat keeps the torso more upright, which usually makes depth easier to reach but harder to hold under heavy load, because the upright torso demands more ankle mobility. A low-bar squat leans the torso forward, which shifts the hips back and makes depth harder to reach for some lifters, but easier to drive out of once you are there. Neither is wrong for depth. But if you switched bar positions recently and your depth disappeared, the bar position is the first suspect, not your mobility. Give the new position a few weeks, test the stance again, and let the setup settle before you conclude the position does not work.

Drills that build depth

Pause squats are the best depth drill there is, because they remove the two things that hide a high squat: momentum and speed. Descend under control, stop at the bottom, hold for two seconds, and drive up. The pause forces you to own the bottom position instead of bouncing through it, and it tells you immediately whether you are actually below parallel — because a two-second hold above parallel feels very different from a two-second hold below it. Program them as your main squat variation for a block: three sets of three to five reps at sixty to seventy percent, with a two-second pause. The weight will feel light. The depth will be honest.

Tempo squats are the second drill, and they work for a different reason: they force control on the way down. A three-second descent removes the dive-bomb, which is the descent that skips the bottom position entirely — the lifter drops fast, the stretch reflex fires, and the squat reverses before it ever reached depth. A slow descent means you feel every inch of the range, and you feel exactly where you stop. Three seconds down, no pause, drive up. Three sets of four to six at sixty to seventy-five percent. The weight is light. The control is the point.

Box squats can build depth, but only if the box is set correctly. A box set too high teaches you to squat high — the body learns the box height as the target, and the target becomes the habit. A box set at the right height, where the hip crease is just below the knee at the touch, teaches the exact bottom position you need. Sit back to the box, touch it lightly, and stand — do not collapse onto it, do not rock off it, and do not let the box do the work of the bottom position. Used that way, the box is a depth gauge, not a crutch. Used the other way, it is the reason your depth disappeared.

Goblet squat holds are the warm-up drill that fixes depth before the bar is even loaded. Hold a kettlebell or dumbbell at your chest, squat as deep as you can, and hold the bottom for thirty seconds while you breathe and let the hips settle. Do it for two or three sets in the warm-up, and use the elbows to push the knees out gently. It opens the hips, it teaches the bottom position without load, and it shows you what your depth looks like when nothing is in the way. If you can hit depth in a goblet squat but not under the bar, the limiter is not your body. It is your setup or your load.

Programming depth work

Depth work belongs in the program the same way any other quality does: as a planned block, not as an afterthought. The most effective approach is a four-to-six-week block where depth is the priority and the load is secondary. Pick one depth drill — pause squats are the usual choice — and make it your main squat variation for the block. Keep the weight at sixty to seventy-five percent, keep the reps at three to five, and film every top set from the side. The rule for the block is simple: a rep that does not hit depth does not count. If the top set comes up high, the weight drops next session. That rule feels harsh, and it is the rule that fixes depth.

The mistake lifters make is trying to fix depth while also chasing a PR. Those two goals conflict, because fixing depth means taking weight off the bar, and chasing a PR means adding it. Pick one. If depth is the problem, depth is the goal, and the PR waits six weeks. The lifter who fixes depth first and then builds back up ends the year stronger than the lifter who kept adding weight to a high squat, because the high squat was never going to count on the platform anyway. A PR that gets red lights is not a PR. It is a gym story.

Frequency helps depth more than intensity does. Squatting three times a week with moderate loads builds the bottom position faster than squatting once a week heavy, because depth is a motor pattern, and motor patterns are built by repetition. Two of those sessions can be light — a tempo squat day and a goblet squat warm-up that carries into a light back squat — and one can be the heavier pause squat day. The total volume stays manageable, the pattern gets grooved three times a week, and the depth that shows up on the heavy day is the depth that was practiced on the light days. Front squat technique and where it fits in a meet cycle is worth reading here too, because the front squat punishes a high squat more than any other variation — the bar falls forward the moment you cut depth, so it is a self-correcting depth drill on its own.

When depth is fixed, build the weight back up slowly, and keep filming. Add five to ten pounds a week to the work sets, and hold the weight for a second week any time depth gets questionable. The film is the judge, not your feeling. A set that looks deep on film counts. A set that feels deep but looks high does not. The lifter who keeps filming after the fix is the lifter who keeps the fix. The lifter who stops filming is the lifter who drifts high again, slowly, over months, and finds out at the next meet.

Depth on meet day

Meet-day depth is a different problem from training depth, because the meet adds three things the gym does not: adrenaline, a max load, and a judging panel you have never lifted in front of. Adrenaline makes everything faster, including the descent, and a fast descent skips depth. The fix is to rehearse the descent speed in training — the same controlled three-second descent you use on tempo squats, applied to the platform. Walk out, set, breathe, descend under control, hit depth, drive. The lifters who rush the descent on the platform are the lifters who trained a rushed descent in the gym.

Attempt selection is the second meet-day lever. Your opener should be a weight you can squat to depth in your sleep — something around ninety percent of your gym max, a weight you have hit to depth a dozen times. The opener exists to get you on the board, not to impress anyone, and a high opener is the most expensive mistake in the sport, because a missed opener on depth puts the whole meet in doubt. The second attempt is the weight you have hit to depth in training. The third is the stretch, and the stretch only works if the first two were clean. Lifters who open too heavy and cut depth do not get a second chance at the first attempt. They get a hole they spend the rest of the meet climbing out of.

Use the warm-up room to check depth, not to test your max. Your last warm-up should be a weight that lets you confirm the bottom position — a single at eighty-five percent, filmed from the side by your handler, checked before you walk out. If the warm-up is high, drop the opener. That is not a defeat. It is the decision that saves the meet. The platform is not the place to discover that your depth is gone. The warm-up room is, and the warm-up room still gives you time to adjust.

One more meet-day point: your weight class does not change your depth, but it changes how much depth costs. A lifter who cut weight to make a class is a lifter with less energy, less leverage, and a harder time hitting the bottom position, because everything about a depleted body resists a deep squat. If you are choosing between classes, the complete guide to powerlifting weight classes and picking yours makes the case for filling out your class instead of cutting down into one — and depth is one more reason that case is right.

The mistakes that keep squats high

Cutting depth as the weight climbs. The most common depth mistake, and the most honest one: the squat is deep at sixty percent and high at ninety, because the body protects itself by shortening the range. The fix is not willpower. The fix is to stop adding weight until depth holds at the current weight, and to treat a high rep as a missed rep. A program that counts high reps as completed reps is a program that is training a high squat.

Confusing butt wink with depth. Butt wink — the pelvis tucking under at the bottom — looks like depth and is not. It is the lower back rounding to buy a few more inches, and those inches cost spinal position and drive out of the hole. Depth comes from the hips and knees, not from the lower back rounding. If your depth appears at the same moment your lower back rounds, you have not found depth. You have found the end of your hip range, and the answer is a stance change or a heel lift, not more rounding.

Bouncing out of the hole. A fast bounce feels powerful, and it can be, but a bounce that reverses the squat above parallel is not a bounce. It is a turnaround that happened too early. Pause squats fix this by removing the bounce entirely and forcing the position to stand on its own. If your paused squat is two inches higher than your bounced squat, the bounce was hiding the gap.

Thinking deeper is always better. Ass-to-grass is a fine goal for a general lifter, but in powerlifting it is wasted range. Every inch below parallel costs energy on the way back up, and the energy spent on those inches is energy not spent on the lockout. The target is an inch below parallel, with control, every rep. Deeper than that is not more correct. It is more expensive.

Fixing depth once and never checking again. Depth drifts. It drifts when the weight climbs, when the stance creeps narrower, when the shoes wear out, when a new bar position settles in. The lifters who keep their depth are the lifters who keep filming, keep checking, and keep treating depth as a skill that needs maintenance. The check takes thirty seconds. The red lights take a whole meet.

Putting it together

Start this week. Film your top set from the side, at hip height, and judge the frame honestly. If the hip crease is below the top of the knee, you have depth — keep filming, keep the margin, and move on. If it is not, you have a diagnosis to make, and the diagnosis is cheap: widen the stance, turn the toes out, try a heeled shoe, and drop ten percent off the bar. One of those four will usually find the depth. Add pause squats as your main variation for the next four to six weeks, three sets of three to five at sixty to seventy percent, with a two-second hold at the bottom. Film every top set. Count only the reps that hit depth.

Write down the stance width and the toe angle that worked, because you will forget them, and the next time depth drifts, the notes are what bring it back. Keep the video habit after the fix, because depth is a skill that needs maintenance, not a problem that stays solved. And when the next meet comes, open light, descend under control, and take the white lights that your training already earned. The squat that counts is not the deepest squat you have ever done. It is the deepest squat you can hit every single time, under the heaviest weight you will ever lift, in front of judges who have seen it all. Train that squat, and depth stops being something you worry about. It becomes something you have.
