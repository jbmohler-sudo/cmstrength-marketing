Simple Food Systems.

Meal prep without the misery — the no-cooking, no-decision food system for lifters who just want the eating handled.

Center Mass Strength

Most nutrition advice for lifters assumes you enjoy cooking. Five-step recipes, Sunday batch sessions, spice blends. If you hate cooking, that advice isn't advice — it's a chore list you'll abandon by Wednesday.

Here's the truth: you don't need recipes. You need a system. A system is a fixed rotation of meals you can assemble in 15 minutes, a grocery list that never changes, and a protein target that gets hit without thinking. Systems don't need motivation. They run.

Start With the Protein Anchor

Every food system starts at the same place: protein. If you're a lifter, you already know the target — roughly 0.7 to 1 gram per pound of body weight a day (see the protein guide). The system just has to make that number unavoidable.

So anchor every meal to a protein source you can get without a recipe:

  • Rotisserie chicken. The single greatest lifter convenience food. A hundred-plus grams of protein for about six dollars, zero cooking, feeds you for two days.
  • Greek yogurt and cottage cheese. Open the container, eat. Twenty-plus grams of protein per serving.
  • Eggs. Ten minutes, any pan, no skill required. Salt counts as seasoning.
  • Deli turkey, canned tuna, canned chicken. Protein that lives in the fridge and pantry, ready in seconds.
  • Protein shakes. Not a meal replacement ideology — just the fastest 25–30 grams on earth when everything else failed.

Pick three of these that you can tolerate daily. That's your protein rotation. Everything else — carbs, fats, vegetables — gets built around it.

The Four-Meal Rotation

You don't need variety. You need four meals you can make half-asleep, eaten on rotation until further notice. Here's a template that covers a training day:

Meal 1 — the breakfast anchor. Greek yogurt, granola, frozen berries. Or eggs and toast. Under five minutes. The rule is simple: protein shows up at breakfast, not just at dinner, because spreading protein across the day is what actually drives muscle growth [1][2].

Meal 2 — the lunch assembly. Deli turkey or rotisserie chicken on bread or in a tortilla, plus a piece of fruit. Ten minutes, eaten at a desk if you have to.

Meal 3 — the training meal. Whatever's fast and familiar before or after the gym: a shake plus a banana, or chicken and microwave rice. (See peri-workout fueling for the timing details.)

Meal 4 — the dinner base. This is the one real plate: a protein, a carb, a vegetable. Which is where the convenience stack comes in.

The Convenience Stack

Dinner is a formula, not a recipe: protein + carb + vegetable, assembled. Keep this standing inventory and dinner makes itself:

Carbs (pick two, always stocked): microwave rice pouches, pasta, oats, bread, tortillas, frozen potatoes. Rice is rice — the microwave pouch is not cheating, it's Tuesday.

Vegetables (frozen, always): frozen broccoli, frozen stir-fry blend, frozen green beans. Frozen vegetables are nutritionally on par with fresh, and the bag that lives in your freezer for three weeks beats the fresh produce you throw away every Sunday [1]. Microwave, salt, done.

The weekly protein cook (optional, 60–90 minutes): if you can tolerate one cooking session a week, make it count — a sheet pan of chicken thighs and a pot of rice covers four dinners. But this is optional. The rotisserie chicken exists for everyone who won't do this, and that's fine.

That's it. Dinner is "chicken thighs, rice, frozen broccoli" or "rotisserie chicken, pasta, stir-fry blend." Different week, same system. Boring is the feature.

Eat the Same Things on Purpose

This is the part people resist: repetition is the system. Every food decision you make is willpower you're not spending on training. Lifters who eat well long-term aren't more disciplined about food — they've made fewer food decisions.

The standing grocery list is what makes it work. Same list every week. Same cart. Same meals. When a meal gets truly unbearable — not boring, unbearable — swap one meal out for another from the same template. Change the cast, never the system.

And the food you don't cook still counts. A rotisserie chicken from the store is cooked food. A microwave rice pouch is rice. Frozen berries are fruit. The idea that "real" eating requires a stove is how the food industry sells you the drive-thru — stop letting it.

The Mistakes That Break the System

  • Starting with a recipe collection. Twenty new recipes is twenty new failure points. Four meals on rotation is a system.
  • The perfect-prep fantasy. The Sunday batch session with eight matching containers lasts until your first busy weekend. The standing grocery list survives it.
  • Skipping breakfast protein. If your protein starts at dinner, you can't catch up. Protein at breakfast is non-negotiable [2].
  • Letting the pantry run dry. A system with no inventory isn't a system, it's a wish. The grocery run is part of the program — same day, same list.
  • Optimizing before it's automatic. Organic, macros to the gram, timing windows — all downstream of actually eating enough protein every day. Get the rotation automatic first.

The Takeaway

Eating like a lifter when you hate cooking is a logistics problem, and the solution is repetition: anchor every meal to no-cook protein, run four base meals on rotation, keep the convenience stack stocked, and stop making food decisions one meal at a time. The boring system you run for a year beats the inspiring meal plan you quit in a week. Food is fuel for the work — handle it like a system, then go train.
