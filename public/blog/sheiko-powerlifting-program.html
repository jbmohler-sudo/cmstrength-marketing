← The Strength Blog SHEIKO POWERLIFTING PROGRAM · THE COMPLETE GUIDE Sheiko Is Boring. That Is Why It Works.

Boris Sheiko's lifters won more world titles than any coach in powerlifting history, and almost none of their training was heavy. Here is the whole system — the numbered blocks, the percentages, and the 16-week cycle.

Center Mass Strength

The Sheiko powerlifting program is the most misunderstood program in the sport, and the misunderstanding is the whole point. A lifter looks at a Sheiko spreadsheet and sees weights that look too light, sets that look too easy, and a schedule that looks like it was written by someone who forgot the heavy days. Then they run it, and three months later their squat moves faster than it ever has, their bench feels like a different lift, and they cannot explain why — because nothing in the program felt hard enough to cause it. That is Sheiko. It is a system that builds maximal strength out of submaximal work, accumulated in such volume that the total does the work the intensity never had to. Boris Sheiko coached the Russian national team to more than forty world championship titles, and his lifters got there by practicing the competition lifts, at weights they could move well, more times than anyone else was willing to.

This guide covers the whole system: who Boris Sheiko is and where the numbered programs come from, the Prilepin's table principle underneath all of it, what #29, #30, #31, #32, and #37 actually are, the 16-week cycle that chains them together, a sample week with the real percentages, who the system fits and who it does not, and the mistakes that turn a good block into a wasted four months. If you are still choosing between the famous programs, the honest comparison of the best powerlifting programs and how to pick the one that fits you puts Sheiko next to 5/3/1, Candito, GZCL, the Texas Method, and Juggernaut. This post is the deep dive on one of them: what Sheiko is, how it works, and how to run it properly.

The short version

Sheiko is high frequency, submaximal weight, and enormous volume. You train the squat, bench press, and deadlift three or four days a week, often two of them in the same session, at mostly 68 to 80 percent of your one-rep max, for many sets of low reps — doubles, triples, fours, and fives — and you never grind a rep. A typical session holds 40 to 60 or more competition-lift reps, and a week of Sheiko #30 holds roughly 60 to 80 total lifts. The percentages come from your real max, not a training max. Sheiko works off your competition one-rep max, which is the opposite of the 5/3/1 approach — and it can afford to, because the weights are light enough that the difference between a true max and a training max does not matter the way it does in a heavy program. The numbered programs are four-week blocks, not a difficulty ladder. #29 prepares, #30 accumulates the most volume, #31 pushes the intensity up, #32 peaks you for the platform, and #37 is a standalone sampler. Run in sequence they make a 16-week cycle. Sheiko is for the intermediate or advanced lifter with accurate maxes, stable technique, and time to recover. It is not a beginner program, it is not a shortcut, and it is not for the lifter who needs every session to feel hard.

Who Boris Sheiko is

Boris Sheiko is a Russian powerlifting coach, the former head coach of the Russian national team, and by most measures the most successful powerlifting coach alive. His athletes have collected more than forty world championship titles, including lifters like Kirill Sarychev, Yury Fedorenko, and Alexey Sivokon — names that held world records in their classes. The numbered programs that carry his name were never published as a commercial system. They started as example blocks in his training books, written for specific athletes at specific levels, and they circulated through forums and spreadsheets in the early 2000s until they became the default way English-speaking lifters met his method. That history matters, because it explains the two things lifters get wrong about Sheiko first: the programs are templates drawn from real coaching, not prescriptions written for you, and the numbers are identifiers for training blocks, not rungs on a ladder. Program #37 is not harder than program #31. Each number is a block with a job, and the job is what matters.

Sheiko's own coaching has moved on since the spreadsheets. His current official route is the Sheiko Gold app, which builds individualized programming from daily readiness, training history, and logged performance — the same adaptive idea CMS is built on, applied to his system. The numbered blocks are the historical route, and they are still the right way to learn the method, because they are fixed, inspectable, and honest about what the system asks of you. What the system asks of you is practice. Sheiko's core belief is that the competition lifts are skills, and skills improve by repetition at weights you can execute cleanly — not by grinding reps at weights that break your technique down. A lifter who squats 70 percent for five sets of five, three times a week, has practiced the squat far more than a lifter who grinds one heavy triple on a Monday and does not touch the bar again until the next Monday. The first lifter's technique gets better every week. The second lifter's technique gets tested every week. Those are not the same thing, and the difference shows up on the platform.

The idea underneath: Prilepin's table

Everything in Sheiko follows from one Soviet-era framework: Prilepin's table, A.S. Prilepin's guidelines for how many reps a lifter should do at a given intensity. The table is simple. At 70 to 80 percent of your max, do 3 to 6 reps per set, for 15 to 24 total reps per exercise. At 80 to 90 percent, do 2 to 4 reps per set, for 10 to 15 total reps. Above 90 percent, do 1 to 2 reps per set, for 4 to 7 total reps. Most of your work lives in the first band. The heavier bands appear sparingly, mainly in the later blocks and on test days. Sheiko built his programs by turning that table into a schedule: the reps, the sets, and the percentages are all chosen so that the total number of lifts — the NL, the number of lifts per session and per week — lands where Prilepin says it should.

That is the number that matters in Sheiko, and it is the number most lifters ignore. The system does not track tonnage or RPE as its primary metric. It tracks how many competition-lift reps you did, and at what average intensity — the coefficient of intensity, the average percentage across all your sets. A session of squats at 70 percent for 5 sets of 5, then bench at 70 percent for 5 sets of 5, then squats again at 75 percent for 4 sets of 4, is a session with a lot of lifts at a moderate intensity, and that is exactly what the system wants: volume high enough to drive adaptation, intensity low enough that every rep is fast and technically clean. The guide to submaximal training and why you should not max out all the time explains the principle in full, and Sheiko is the program that principle built — the most committed version of it in powerlifting. The weights feel light. They are supposed to. The test is not whether the session felt hard. The test is whether the bar moved faster in week twelve than it did in week one.

There are no AMRAP sets in Sheiko. No set is taken to failure, and no set is prescribed as "as many as you can." Every set has a number, and the number is the program. That is a feature, not a gap: a lifter who never grinds a rep never practices bad technique under fatigue, never accumulates the kind of fatigue that costs the next session, and never has to guess whether they had one more rep in them. The cost is that the program gives you no daily feedback loop — there is no plus set to tell you the training max is right. The feedback is the cycle: you run the block, you test at the end, and the test tells you whether the system worked. Lifters who need a weekly number to feel like they are progressing will hate that. Lifters who can wait four weeks for the answer will get it.

The numbered programs: #29, #30, #31, #32, #37

The five numbers you will meet are four-week blocks, each with a phase and a job. The standard competition preparation chains four of them into a 16-week cycle, and the fifth is a standalone entry point.

ProgramPhaseDurationJob
#29Preparation 14 weeksModerate volume and intensity; builds work capacity and teaches the structure
#30Preparation 24 weeksHighest volume in the cycle, about 50 percent more than #29; the hardest block
#31Transmutation4 weeksVolume comes down, intensity goes up; weights reach 85 to 90 percent
#32Peaking / realization4 weeksLowest volume, highest intensity; max test day, then a taper to competition
#37Standalone4 weeksGeneral strength at moderate volume; the sampler for lifters new to Sheiko

Read the table by the sequence, because the sequence is the program. #29 is the entry point: lower total volume than the later blocks, moderate intensity, and the block that teaches you what a Sheiko week feels like — three or four sessions, multiple competition lifts per session, and a lot of sets that look too easy on paper. #30 is the block the whole cycle is built around. It carries roughly 50 percent more volume than #29, and it is the hardest block, not because the weights are heavy but because the number of lifts is high and the recovery cost accumulates across the four weeks. #31 shifts the balance: volume comes down, intensity climbs into the 85 to 90 percent range, and the work starts to look like the meet. #32 is the peak: the lowest volume, the highest intensity, a max test day in week thirteen, and a three-week taper that brings you to the platform fresh. #37 sits outside the chain. It is a four-week standalone block at moderate volume, and it is the right first Sheiko program for a lifter who wants to try the method without committing to a 16-week cycle.

Two warnings about the numbers. First, the labels you will see online — "beginner," "intermediate," "advanced" — are translations and community classifications, not a complete coaching prescription. #29 is the usual starting point because its volume is lower, and #32 demands the most recovery, but the numbers themselves are example blocks from Sheiko's books, written for specific athletes, and different files circulating online can carry different volume profiles. Check the block you are running against the phase it is supposed to serve, and do not assume a higher number means a harder program. Second, some sources describe #32 as an off-season recovery block rather than a peaking block. That is a different file from a different context. In the #29-to-#32 competition sequence, #32 is the peaking block with the test day and the taper. If the #32 you have does not have a max test and a taper, you have a different block than the one this cycle uses, and you should not run it as the peak.

The 16-week cycle, block by block

Chained together, the four blocks make a 16-week competition preparation, and the shape of it is the shape of every good periodization: build the base, accumulate the most work, convert the work into intensity, then realize it on the platform. The complete guide to powerlifting periodization and how training blocks fit together shows the structure underneath, and Sheiko is that structure drawn in the most patient way possible — four weeks per block, sixteen weeks per cycle, and no shortcut between the base and the peak.

Weeks 1 to 4 — #29, preparation. The volume is moderate, the intensity is moderate, and the job is to build work capacity and teach the lifter the rhythm of the system. Most of the work sits at 70 to 80 percent. The sessions are long but not crushing, and the lifter finishes the block feeling like they trained a lot without ever being wrecked. That feeling is the point: #29 is the block that proves you can handle the volume, and if you cannot handle #29, you cannot handle what comes next.

Weeks 5 to 8 — #30, accumulation. This is the hardest block in the cycle, and it is hard in the Sheiko way: the volume jumps about 50 percent over #29, the sessions get longer, and the number of lifts per week climbs to roughly 60 to 80. The intensity stays mostly in the 70 to 80 percent band, with occasional work at 85 to 90 percent. The fatigue accumulates quietly — the weights never feel heavy, but the total work does, and by the end of the block the lifter is tired in a way that a heavy program never makes them tired: not sore, not beaten up, just worn down by the sheer number of quality reps. That is the adaptation the cycle is built on. The lifter who survives #30 has built the base that #31 and #32 will convert into a total.

Weeks 9 to 12 — #31, transmutation. Volume comes down, intensity goes up. The work moves more often into the 80 to 90 percent band, the sets get shorter, and the sessions start to feel like the meet. This is the block where the accumulated practice turns into strength you can feel: the same weights that moved at 70 percent in #29 now move faster, and the heavier sets in #31 confirm it. Do not add volume here to chase the feeling of #30. The volume is coming down on purpose, and the intensity is doing the work now.

Weeks 13 to 16 — #32, peaking and realization. The lowest volume and the highest intensity in the cycle. Week thirteen holds a max test day, and the remaining three weeks taper down to the competition. Singles at 90 percent and above appear here, and almost nowhere else in the system — Sheiko rarely programs above 90 percent in standard training blocks, and when he does, it is at the end, for a reason. The taper is not a rest. It is the block working as designed: the fatigue from #30 and #31 dissipates, the strength they built stays, and the lifter arrives at the platform with the most practice and the least fatigue of the whole cycle. A lifter who skips the taper and keeps training hard into the meet is a lifter who throws away the last four weeks of the cycle. Take the taper. It is the peak.

A sample week: Sheiko #30

Here is what a week looks like in #30, the highest-volume block, using the published first-week structure. Three training days, each session holding multiple competition lifts, and every percentage taken from your competition one-rep max — not a training max.

DayExerciseSets × Reps% of 1RM
MondaySquat5 × 570%
MondayBench press5 × 570%
MondaySquat4 × 475%
MondayBench press4 × 575%
WednesdayDeadlift4 × 380%
WednesdayBench press5 × 380%
FridaySquat5 × 382%
FridayBench press5 × 285%

Two things to notice. First, the bench press appears in every session. That is normal for Sheiko: the bench is trained three or four times a week, because the bench responds to frequency and the volume is spread thin enough that the shoulders and elbows can take it. Second, Friday is the week's highest-intensity day — squats at 82 percent, bench at 85 percent — and it is also the day where the reps per set drop. That is Prilepin's table in action: as the intensity climbs, the reps per set fall, and the total number of lifts stays in the band the table calls for. The week holds roughly 60 to 80 total lifts across the three sessions, which is far more than most Western programs prescribe — and that is the whole method, in one week.

Read the percentages carefully, because they are the part lifters get wrong most often. Every percentage in Sheiko is a share of your competition one-rep max — your true maximum, tested or estimated honestly. There is no training max in the system. That is a deliberate choice, and it is the opposite of the 5/3/1 rule: Wendler discounts the max by 10 percent because his program puts you under heavy weights every third week, and a heavy program needs the buffer. Sheiko does not need the buffer, because the weights are submaximal by design — the program can afford to work off the real max, because 70 percent of a real max is still a weight you can move well. The complete 5/3/1 powerlifting program guide and how its training max works shows the other approach, and the contrast is the lesson: the training max exists to protect you from heavy weights, and Sheiko does not need one, because its weights are never heavy enough to need protecting from. The protection in Sheiko is the volume, and the volume is the thing that breaks lifters who misjudge their maxes. If your max is inflated, every percentage is inflated with it, and a program that is supposed to be submaximal becomes a program that grinds. Test your max honestly, or estimate it from a heavy set of three to five reps, and then trust the percentages. A max you can hit for a clean single is a max you can build on. A max you hit once, on a great day, with a spotter pulling the bar, is a max that will wreck the cycle by week six.

Who Sheiko is for — and who it is not for

Sheiko is for the intermediate or advanced lifter with accurate maxes, stable technique, time to recover, and a temperament that can handle a program that feels too easy for the first month. One to three years of consistent training at minimum, a real base of strength, and a schedule that allows three or four long sessions a week — the sessions run long, because the sets are many, and a lifter who has ninety minutes three days a week will struggle to finish the work. It is for the lifter who has stalled on programs that asked for heavy weights every week, and who suspects the answer is more practice, not more intensity. It is for the lifter with a meet date sixteen weeks out, because the cycle is built to land on a platform.

It is also, honestly, a good program for lifters over 40 who meet the other conditions — and the reasons are the same reasons it works for anyone, but they matter more with age. The weights are submaximal, so the joints take less pounding; nothing is ever ground out, so the technique never breaks down under a weight the lifter cannot control; and the progression is built on volume, not on weekly max attempts, so there is no week where the program demands a number the body cannot give. A 45-year-old lifter with a job, a family, and joints that remember every bad decision needs a program that respects recovery as a variable, not as an excuse. Sheiko does, by design — but only if the lifter respects the volume. The recovery cost in Sheiko is not the weight. It is the number of lifts, and an older lifter who runs #30 at full volume without the sleep and the food to support it will find that out in week six. The complete guide to a powerlifting program for beginners covers the phase before this one, and the handoff matters: a true beginner, under a year of consistent training, should run linear progression first, adding weight every session, because that is the fastest progress a beginner will ever make. Sheiko is the program after that, not instead of it.

Sheiko is not for the beginner. The volume is too high, the percentages assume a max that a beginner does not have yet, and the program gives no daily feedback — a beginner needs to see the weight go up every session to learn what progress feels like, and Sheiko does not give them that. It is not for the lifter with a meet in eight weeks, because the cycle is sixteen weeks long and cutting it in half throws away the base the peak is built on. It is not for the lifter who trains twice a week, because the frequency is the program — three or four sessions is the minimum, and two sessions cannot hold the volume. It is not for the lifter who needs every session to feel hard, because the sessions will not feel hard, and that lifter will add weight, or add sets, or add a fourth lift, and break the program in the first month. And it is not for the lifter who cannot recover from volume — the lifter whose sleep is short, whose food is thin, or whose stress is high will find that Sheiko's cost is not the bar, it is the total number of lifts, and the log will show it before the mirror does. The honest test is the same as for any program: run a full block. Did the numbers go up? Then it works, whatever is inside it. The complete guide to RPE for powerlifting and rating your sets honestly is the tool for that test, and it cuts against Sheiko in one useful way: a set that felt like a 7 when it was a 9 is a set that will cost you next week, and the log is what tells you whether the program is working or whether you are lying to it.

How to run it for 16 weeks

Picking the program is the easy part. Running it for sixteen weeks is the work, and the rules are simpler than the spreadsheet looks.

First, start with #29 or #37, not #30 and never #32. The most common way lifters break Sheiko is by starting with the hardest block because it looks like the real program. #29 is the entry point: lower volume, the same structure, and four weeks to learn what a Sheiko week feels like before the volume jumps. #37 is the other honest entry point — a standalone sampler for a lifter who wants to try the method without committing to the full cycle. Either one teaches you the rhythm. #30 does not teach you the rhythm. It tests whether you learned it.

Second, run the block as written for the full four weeks before changing anything. The percentages are the program, and the sets and reps are the program, and changing them because the weights feel light is the same mistake as adding volume to a program that is working. The first block is orientation: you are learning the structure, the weights are estimates, and the numbers are noisy. The second block is the test: the volume is higher, the structure is familiar, and the results are real. A lifter who changes a block after one week has not tested it. They have tried it on, and a program is not a shirt. If you want a program that adjusts to how you actually recover instead of to a fixed spreadsheet — the volume set from your real numbers, the sessions adjusted to your actual fatigue — that is what adaptive programming does, and it is the same principles as Sheiko, with the adjustments made for you instead of by you.

Third, track the number of lifts. The NL is the metric the system is built on, and the log is the only place it lives. Write down every set, every rep, and every percentage, and count the lifts per session and per week. The count tells you whether you are actually running Sheiko or just running a program that looks like it — and it tells you whether the fatigue you feel in week six of #30 is the program working or the program breaking you. The complete guide to an intermediate powerlifting program and the 4-day template covers the handoff from beginner to intermediate in detail, and Sheiko is one of the best programs to hand off to — the structure is fixed enough to follow on a tired morning, and the progression is slow enough to sustain for sixteen weeks.

Fourth, protect the recovery. The volume is the cost, and the cost is paid in sleep, food, and time between sessions. A Sheiko week holds 60 to 80 competition lifts, and that is before the accessory work — the rows, the single-leg work, the core work that supports the three lifts. Eat enough to support it, sleep enough to recover from it, and keep the accessory work where it belongs: supporting the main lifts, not competing with them. Fifty to a hundred total reps of assistance per session, at a weight you control, is the range. More is not better. More is just more. Conditioning fits around the program, not inside it: walking, sled work, easy bike rides, on the days between sessions, kept easy enough that it does not cost you the next lifting day.

Fifth, take the deload when the cycle gives you one, and take the taper at the end. There is no deload week inside a Sheiko block — the blocks are four weeks of accumulating work, and the relief comes when the volume drops in #31 and the taper runs in #32. That is the structure, and a lifter who adds a deload week in the middle of #30 because it feels hard is a lifter who is running a different program. The guide to deloads vs one-day adjustments and when to back off covers how to tell a planned back-off from a bad day, and the short version is this: a bad day is one session, and the answer to a bad day is to finish the session at the prescribed weights, not to add a rest week the cycle did not plan.

The mistakes that turn Sheiko bad

Starting with #30 or #32. The most common mistake, and the one that causes most of the others. #30 is the hardest block in the cycle, and #32 is the peak. Starting with either one is starting with the test before the preparation, and the volume will break a lifter who has not adapted to the method. Start with #29 or #37. The cost of starting too easy is four weeks of light training. The cost of starting too hard is a wasted cycle.

Inflating your maxes. Every percentage in Sheiko is a share of your competition one-rep max, and an inflated max inflates every set in the program. A lifter who claims a 500-pound squat they hit once, on a great day, with a spotter's hands on the bar, is a lifter whose 70 percent is really 80 percent, and whose 80 percent is really a grind — and a program that grinds is not Sheiko. Test honestly, or estimate from a heavy set of three to five reps, and trust the numbers. The program only works if the percentages are true.

Adding weight because it feels light. The weights feel light because they are supposed to. Adding ten pounds to every set because the first week was easy is the fastest way to turn a submaximal program into a maximal one, and a maximal program with Sheiko's volume is a program that will injure you. The volume is the load. The weight is the vehicle. Leave the weight alone and let the volume do the work.

Adding volume to a working program. The blocks already hold 60 to 80 lifts a week. Adding a fourth lift, or extra sets, or a fifth day, because the program feels too easy in week two, is the fastest way to break a program that was working. If it is working, leave it alone. If it is not working after a full block, change one thing, not five, and give it another block before judging.

Skipping the taper. The last three weeks of #32 are the peak, and they work by letting the fatigue from #30 and #31 dissipate while the strength stays. A lifter who keeps training hard into the meet is a lifter who throws away the last four weeks of the cycle. Take the taper. It is the whole point of the sixteen weeks.

Program-hopping. Six weeks of Sheiko, then six weeks of 5/3/1, then six weeks of Candito is not a year of training. It is three first cycles, and first cycles do not build totals. The lifters with the biggest totals are usually the lifters who ran the same program, or the same family of programs, for years. Sheiko's lifters ran Sheiko for years. Boredom is not a reason to switch. A stall after a full cycle is. The complete Smolov squat program guide and who should run it is the contrast worth knowing: Smolov is a thirteen-week squat specialization that is brutally intense by design, and it is the opposite of Sheiko in almost every way — short, sharp, and narrow, where Sheiko is long, patient, and complete. A lifter who wants a squat peak runs Smolov. A lifter who wants a total runs Sheiko.

Running the wrong block for the goal. #37 is a sampler, not a peaking block. Running #37 for sixteen weeks is not running Sheiko — it is running the same four weeks four times, and the program has nothing new to give you after the second run. The blocks have jobs, and the cycle has a shape, and the shape is what makes it work. If you want a peak, run the chain. If you want a sampler, run #37 once, then decide. The complete Russian squat program guide, week by week is another contrast: a six-week squat cycle built on the same Soviet volume tradition, narrower and shorter than Sheiko, and a good way to see the family resemblance — the same submaximal weights, the same accumulation, the same patience, aimed at one lift instead of three.

Putting it together

Start this week. Pick your entry point — #29 if you are committing to the cycle, #37 if you want the sampler first — and write down the weights for the first block from your honest competition maxes: mostly 70 to 80 percent, sets of three to five, three or four sessions a week, every set prescribed and none taken to failure. Run the block as written for four full weeks. Write down every number: the weight, the sets, the reps, and the total number of lifts per week. In four weeks, compare the numbers, not the feeling, and move to the next block if the structure held. In sixteen weeks, test — and the test is the answer, not the feeling. If the numbers moved, the program worked, and the next cycle starts from stronger maxes and the same patient structure. If they did not, the answer is in the log: the maxes, the volume, or the recovery, and the fix is one change, not a new program.

Sheiko has lasted since the early 2000s in English, and decades longer in Russian, because it is honest about what a program can do. It cannot make you strong in six weeks. It can make you stronger, block after block, for years, if you run it as written and let the volume compound. That is not an exciting promise. It is a true one, and a true promise kept for sixteen weeks is worth more than an exciting promise broken in six. The program is boring. That is the whole point. Run it, write it down, and let the bar tell you the truth.
